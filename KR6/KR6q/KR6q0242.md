# Sān gēn zuò chán shuō 三根坐禪說 (Japanese: *Sangon zazensetsu*)

*Exposition of Seated Meditation for the Three Capacities* — a short one-juan Japanese Sōtō Zen text by [[瑩山|Keizan]] 瑩山紹瑾 (Keizan Jōkin, 1264–1325), the second great founder of the Japanese Sōtō school and direct dharma-heir of [[徹通義介|Tettsū Gikai]] 徹通義介, grand-dharma-heir of [[道元|Dōgen]]. Keizan's posthumous imperial title: *Sōji Emmei Kokushi* 總持圓明國師 ("National Master of Total-Retention and Perfect-Brightness"), from which the text attributes him as *Zǒng chí Yuán míng guó shī Yíngshān*.

## About the work

A short one-juan text on meditation-practice for practitioners at three different spiritual levels, G84 n2086. Non-commentary; *commentedTextid* omitted.

Keizan distinguishes three capacities of practitioner:

1. **Upper capacity** (*shàng gēn* 上根): The most advanced practitioners — not even aware of the Buddhas' appearing-in-the-world, not realising the subtle un-transmitted truth of the Buddha-patriarchs. "When hungry eat food, when tired sleep." Sitting openly-and-naturally without distinguishing awakened-from-unawakened.

2. **Middle capacity** (*zhōng gēn* 中根): Those at the standard Sōtō meditation-practice level. Letting go of all affairs, resting all conditions; throughout the twelve hours of the day sustaining effortful attention. Taking up a *huà tóu* 話頭 (*kōan*), focussing attention at the tip of the nose, realising that the original face does not pass through birth-and-death, comprehending that the Buddha-nature does not fall into conceptual-analytical differentiation.

3. **Lower capacity** (*xià gēn* 下根): Beginners. Valuing simply the establishment of karmic connections; distinguishing good-and-evil actions; manifesting Buddha-nature directly through mind. Specific postural and sensory instructions: feet in lotus-position, hands in meditation-*mudra*, mouth closed like a seam, eyes not too wide and not too narrow, ears not listening to good-or-bad sounds, nose not smelling pleasant-or-unpleasant odours, body not leaning on any object, movement stopped, mind not going after external objects, joy and sorrow both ceased.

Keizan's closing position: even minimal advancement in seated meditation exceeds the merit of constructing hundreds of thousands of monastery-stupas — "the most excellent actual-practice among the ten thousand practices is the single gate of seated meditation alone."

### Abstract

[[瑩山|Keizan Jōkin]] 瑩山紹瑾 (1264–1325): the second great founder of Japanese Sōtō, succeeding Dōgen (d. 1253) → [[懷奘|Ejō]] 懷奘 → [[徹通義介|Gikai]] 義介 → Keizan. Founded **Sōji-ji** 總持寺 in 1321, which became (alongside Eihei-ji) one of the two principal head-monasteries of the Japanese Sōtō school. Keizan's doctrinal-institutional work — particularly his *[[傳光錄|Denkōroku]]* 傳光錄 (*Record of the Transmission of the Light*) and his institutionalisation of Sōtō monastic practice at Sōji-ji — transformed Sōtō from Dōgen's intimate teaching-circle into a national institutional tradition.

Dating: *notBefore* c. 1290 (Keizan's mature productive period); *notAfter* 1325 (his death). Precise composition-date not preserved in the received text.

## Other points of interest

The *Sān gēn zuò chán shuō* is one of Keizan's many short texts establishing clear practical-meditation guidelines for his expanding Sōtō community. The text's three-tier capacity structure — addressing practitioners at distinct developmental stages — was pedagogically revolutionary in its era, providing a unified framework that could accommodate both beginners and advanced practitioners within a single institutional practice-programme.
