# Shíwū Qīnggǒng chánshī yǔlù 石屋清洪禪師語錄

Two-juan Yuán-dynasty *yǔlù* of **Shíwū Qīnggǒng** [[清珙]] 石屋清珙 (1272 – Zhìzhèng 12.7.21 / late summer 1352, *shì* 81, *là* 54), Yángqí-branch Línjì master, dharma-heir of Jíān Zōngxìn 及菴宗信 (of the Dàochǎng / Dàochǎngxìn line); posthumously styled Fócí Huìzhào chánshī 佛慈慧照禪師. *Xuzangjing* X70 n1399. (*Catalog typographical slip:* the Kanripo catalog title reads 清**洪** [hóng 水+共] where the standard form per DILA and most sources — as well as the preface's own title-line "*Fúyuán Shíwū **Gǒng** chánshī yǔlù*" — gives 清**珙** [gǒng 玉+共]. The variant 清洪 is attested in some traditional bibliographies. Here the catalog form is preserved in the frontmatter with the conventional reading given in prose.) Compiled by his dharma-heir [[至柔]] Zhìróu *děngbiān* (Jiāxīngzàng recension). Preface by an unnamed Yuán literatus; the *xíngzhuàng* appended to juan 2 is the main biographical source.

### Abstract

Per DILA and the *xíngzhuàng*: Qīnggǒng (surname Wēn 溫) was a native of Chángshú 常熟 in Sūzhōu (Jiāngsū). Tonsured at twenty under Yǒngwéi 永惟 of Xīngjiào Chóngfúsì; fully ordained three years later. First studied with Gāofēng Yuánmiào 高峰原妙 at Tiānmùshān for three years; was sent by him (with the famous pun on his "blind-donkey" self-deprecation) to Jíān Zōngxìn at Xīfēng in Wēnzhōu, who confirmed his awakening. Thereafter he climbed Xiáwùshān 霞霧山 and built a thatched hut named Tiānhú 天湖 where he cultivated alone — cutting his own firewood, composing the spare nature-poetry for which he is famous in later Korean and Japanese reception. In Yuántǒng *xīnwèi* (1331), invited to found the Fúyuán chánshā 福源禪剎 at Dānghú in Jiāhé (Jiāxīng) as second-generation abbot. Seven years later he retired back to Tiānhú. During the Zhìzhèng era he declined imperial summons; the emperor sent *jīnlán* robes and the empress scarlet cloth. He died at Zhìzhèng 12 *rénchén* autumn 7.21 (1352), aged 81, *là* 54. His Korean dharma-heir Táigǔ Bou 太古普愚 — who famously received a "gold-scaled fish on a straight hook" verse of transmission — returned to Koryŏ to found the Taego-jong 太古宗, which remains one of the two principal Korean Sŏn lineages into the modern period; King Kongmin of Koryŏ venerated Qīnggǒng as National Preceptor and collected his relics for a Korean reliquary.

The *yǔlù* (also circulating under the Jiāxīngzàng designation *Shíwū chánshī yǔlù*) preserves *shàngtáng* 上堂 and *shìzhòng* 示眾 materials from the Fúyuán abbacy, plus the celebrated *shānjū shī* 山居詩 sequence in the manner of Hánshān 寒山 that anchors his later literary fame.

## Translations and research

Substantial secondary literature in Korean (dozens of studies on Taego Bou and his master Qīng-gǒng) and in Japanese (Shiwu included in *Gozan bungaku* studies). In English, selections of the *shān-jū shī* have been translated by Red Pine (Bill Porter), *The Zen Works of Stonehouse: Poems and Talks of a 14th-Century Chinese Hermit* (San Francisco: Mercury House, 1999; second edition Copper Canyon, 2009) — the standard English-language treatment.
