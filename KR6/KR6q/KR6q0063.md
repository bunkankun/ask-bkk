# Dàhuì Pǔjué chánshī niánpǔ 大慧普覺禪師年譜

*Chronological Biography of Chán Master Dàhuì Pǔjué*

compiled (*biān* 編) by [[祖詠]] Zǔyǒng; prefaced (*xù* 序) by [[張掄]] Liánshè jūshì in Chúnxī 10.4.15 (6 May 1183); afterwarded (*bá* 跋) with emendations by [[宗演]] Huázàng Zōngyǎn in Kāixǐ 1 (1205)

## About the work

A one-juan chronological biography (*niánpǔ* 年譜) of [[宗杲]] Dàhuì Zōnggǎo (1089–1163), structured year by year from Yuányòu 4 (1089) through Lóngxīng 1 (1163), with each entry a short prose summary of Zōnggǎo's movements, encounters, sermons delivered, correspondence, and personal circumstance in the given year. The standard source for Zōnggǎo's chronology and the proximate basis for all subsequent *niánpǔ* work on him in modern scholarship.

### Abstract

[[祖詠]] Zǔyǒng, a direct disciple of Zōnggǎo, compiled the *niánpǔ* during a period of residence at Línān, apparently completed in or slightly before 1183. The text opens with the layman [[張掄]] Zhāng Lún's preface dated 淳熙癸卯四月望日 — the full-moon day of the fourth lunar month of Chúnxī 10, i.e. 6 May 1183 — which frames the compilation as a response to a request from Jìng'ān [[黃汝霖]] Huáng Rǔlín 敬菴黃汝霖, whose ancestor had been a room-student of Dàhuì. Zhāng Lún, a prolific lay-Buddhist song-writer self-styled Liánshè jūshì 蓮社居士, was a figure of cultural influence in the 1180s court of Xiàozōng.

The closing afterword by Huázàng [[宗演]] Zōngyǎn 華藏宗演, dated 開禧乙丑 (Kāixǐ 1 = 1205), supplies the critical philological note on the text: Zōngyǎn declares that Zǔyǒng's original draft was admirable but contained errors and lacunae (*bùnéng wú wù tuō* 不能無誤脫) which he had long regretted. Later, on obtaining a manuscript by Jiāngxī Yíng Yúnwò 江西瑩雲臥 (a figure identified with Yúnwò [[演教]] Yǎnjiào 雲臥演教, Zōnggǎo's attendant during the Héngyáng–Méizhōu exile years), whose corrections aligned with Zōngyǎn's earlier suspicions, Zōngyǎn himself collated the two (at leisure 青山無事 "at the green mountain, having no business") and introduced more than sixty emendations. The received Jiāxīng-canon text is therefore Zōngyǎn's 1205 revision of Zǔyǒng's 1183 original. Funding for the block-cutting came from [[包檉芳]] Bāo Chéngfāng of Jiāxīng, who donated ten *liǎng* of silver for the printing (recorded in the final colophon).

Dating follows the received-recension principle: notBefore 1183 (Zhāng Lún's preface to the original compilation); notAfter 1205 (Zōngyǎn's afterword sealing the collated recension). Dynasty 宋 per the catalog meta, specifically Southern Sòng.

The *niánpǔ*'s principal evidentiary value is biographical. Each year's entry quotes extensively from Zōnggǎo's own letters, lecture records, and incidental verse, so that the text is in effect a dossier of primary-source citations structured chronologically. This makes it indispensable for the reconstruction of Zōnggǎo's movements in the politically complex decades of the 1130s–1150s — the years of the Jiànyán flight, the Shàoxīng peace-party politics, Zōnggǎo's fifteen-year exile after [[張九成]] Zhāng Jiǔchéng's implication in the 1141 crackdown, and the triumphant late abbacies at Yùwáng and Jìngshān.

## Translations and research

- No complete English translation. The *niánpǔ* is universally cited in modern Dàhuì scholarship (Schlütter 2008; Levering, various articles; Araki Kengo's Japanese corpus) for chronological reconstruction.
- Ishii Shūdō 石井修道's *Sōdai zenshūshi no kenkyū* (1987) and Araki Kengo 荒木見悟's Dàhuì studies rely heavily on the *niánpǔ*.
- Miriam Levering has used the *niánpǔ* in her reconstruction of Dàhuì's lay female patronage and of his biographical self-representation.
- The closest thing to an edited modern version is Kimura Shizuo 木村靜雄's Japanese-annotated text, incorporated into larger Dàhuì research collections.

## Other points of interest

The *niánpǔ* as a Buddhist biographical sub-genre is itself a Southern-Sòng Chán innovation: adapted from the late-Northern-Sòng Confucian *niánpǔ* tradition (exemplified by the *Huángjí niánpǔ* 黃極年譜 and similar annalistic biographies of literati), its application to the life of a Chán master imports a literati-historical apparatus into Chán biographical writing, replacing (or supplementing) the older hagiographic genres of the *shèng sēng zhuàn* and the lamp-record. Zǔyǒng's *Dàhuì niánpǔ* is among the earliest and most influential Chán *niánpǔ*; the genre subsequently flourished in the late Sòng and Yuán for major abbots and became a standard biographical instrument of the Chán institutional tradition.
