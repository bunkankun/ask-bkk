# Chuán-guāng lù 傳光録
*Record of the Transmission of the Light*
by [[瑩山]] Keizan Jōkin (語)

## About the work

A two-fascicle **transmission-of-the-lamp record** by [[瑩山]] **Keizan Jōkin** (1268–1325), the second great patriarch (Sōji-ji-line) of the Japanese Sōtō-Zen school after [[道元]] **Dōgen** (1200–1253). Composed in **kana-majiri** (mixed Chinese-Japanese script) at **Yōkō-ji** 永光寺 in Noto, c. **1300–1325**, the *Denkō-roku* presents the dharma-transmission as a series of fifty-three biographical-and-doctrinal sketches, beginning with the Buddha's transmission to Mahākāśyapa and proceeding through the twenty-eight Indian patriarchs, the Six Chinese Patriarchs, the Cáodòng-school masters down to Tiāntóng Rújìng [[如淨]] (Dōgen's master), and finally Dōgen and Dōgen's heir Koun Ejō [[懷奘]] (Keizan's grandfather-in-dharma).

### Abstract

The principal preface (序) by **Mu'in** 無隱 (a "*tudāra*" — undocumented elder), which prefaces the Edo-period *editio princeps*, identifies the work and its author:

*"Since [the Buddha] raised the gold-flower and overturned the *kshatra-pole* — between West-India and East-Cathay — the dharma was passed down with its robe and the lamps did not break for thirty-three persons; these are called *patriarchs*. Below the patriarchs the robe stayed [in China] and was not transmitted [to Japan separately]; the dharma spread throughout the *sand-realms*. From this, the **Five-House masters** — each holding the *spirit-snake's pearl* and each embracing the *Jingshan jewel* — what is called the **Treasury of the True Dharma Eye**, also named the **Great Light-Treasure**. Vast! Great indeed! Our Sōji-ji founder Bùtsu-ji Zenji **Keizan**, the great-Reverend, once carried the *no-character* seal and brought down the *no-tongue* talk; turning to the *unseen-crown-mark* of the patriarchs from above, he gave each of them the *eye-dot*. He named it the **Record of the Transmission of the Light**. This is the *interior secret-text* of the Mahāyāna [Buddhist] *room*."*

The text proper is structured as **fifty-three chapters**, each devoted to one patriarch. Each chapter opens with a brief biographical sketch (in kana), then the *kōan-encounter* (機縁 *kien*) of that patriarch's awakening, then Keizan's explanatory commentary (in kanbun) on the dharma-content of that encounter, and closes with a concluding *jì-sòng* (偈頌) verse summarising the matter. The structure is modelled on the Sòng *transmission-of-the-lamp* tradition (*Jǐng-dé chuán-dēng-lù*, *Jiā-tài pǔ-dēng-lù*, etc.) but is far more *pedagogically* organised: each chapter is essentially a stand-alone teaching-text that can be used in a single training session.

The biographical preface from the Edo *editio princeps* records Keizan's life-history: born **Bun'ei 5 (1268)** in **Tane-gun, Echizen province** 越前州多禰郡 (modern Echizen-shi, Fukui), lay surname **Fujiwara** 藤. His mother dreamt of swallowing sunlight before conceiving him. Tonsured at age 8 under **Tettsū Gikai** [[徹通]] 徹通義介, ordained at age 13 under **Koun Ejō** [[懷奘]]. After awakening at age 22 under Tettsū Gikai on the *Heijōshin-kore-dō* ("ordinary mind is the Way") kōan of Zhàozhōu, he became abbot of Jōman-ji 城滿寺 in Awa, then succeeded Tettsū at **Daijō-ji** 大乘寺 in Kaga. Founded **Yōkō-ji** 永光寺 in Noto and **Sōji-ji** 總持寺, both in Noto (modern Ishikawa). Died 1325. Posthumous title **Bùtsu-ji Zenji** 佛慈禪師 with later additions **Sōji Enmei Kokushi** 總持圓明國師.

The dating bracket reflects: the work was composed in the period of Keizan's mature teaching at Yōkō-ji (c. 1300–1325). The Taishō recension is the Edo-period printing.

The work has been **canonical** for the Sōji-ji line of Japanese Sōtō-Zen — institutionally complementary to Dōgen's [[KR6t0288]] *Shōbōgenzō*. As Mu'in's preface observes: *"It is in mutual surface-and-back with the [[KR6t0288|Eihei kōso shōbōgenzō]]."* Where Dōgen's *Shōbōgenzō* lays out the doctrinal philosophy of Sōtō-Zen, the *Denkō-roku* lays out the **dharma-lineage** as a sequence of teaching-encounters.

## Translations and research

The principal English translation is **Francis Dōjun Cook**, *The Record of Transmitting the Light: Zen Master Keizan's Denkōroku* (Wisdom Publications, 1991, repr. 2003) — complete, with extensive scholarly apparatus. Earlier partial translations include **Thomas Cleary**, *Transmission of Light* (Weatherhill, 1990).

For Keizan's biography and the Sōji-ji-line context, see **William M. Bodiford**, *Sōtō Zen in Medieval Japan* (Univ. of Hawai'i Press, 1993), chs. 5–6; **Bodiford**, "Sōtō Zen in a Japanese Town: Field Notes on a Once-Every-Thirty-Three-Years Kannon Festival," *Japanese Journal of Religious Studies* 21 (1994). Recent monograph: **Steven Heine**, *Like Cats and Dogs: Contesting the Mu Kōan in Zen Buddhism* (Oxford UP, 2014), discussing Keizan's kōan-pedagogy.

## Other points of interest

The *Denkō-roku* is the principal source for the **traditional Sōtō-Zen dharma-line** as reconstructed in medieval Japan — the line that the Sōji-ji Sōtō clergy still recite at funerals and ceremonies. The fact that Dōgen and Ejō appear as the closing two patriarchs of the line emphasises the *successorship* of Keizan: the *Denkō-roku* is in part Keizan's claim to be the **next** transmission after them. Modern Sōtō historiography treats Keizan as the institutional founder of Sōtō as a mass-Buddhist school in Japan (Dōgen having confined his teaching to a small monastic elite).
