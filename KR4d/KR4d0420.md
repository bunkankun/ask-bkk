# Yí-shān jí 遺山集
*The Yí-shān Collection*
by [[元好問]] (撰)

## About the work

The collected works in forty *juàn* of Yuán Hǎo-wèn 元好問 (CBDB 10074, 1190–1257), *zì* Yù-zhī 裕之, *hào* Yí-shān 遺山 — the foremost Jīn-dynasty literary figure and one of the great Chinese poets, the central anthologist of Jīn-period verse through his *Zhōng-zhōu jí* 中州集 and *Zhōng-zhōu yuè-fǔ* 中州樂府, and the foundational historian of the Jīn through his manuscript *Rén-jīn lèi* 壬辰雜編 which Tuō-tuō's *Jīn-shǐ* compilers later drew on. A *Xián-tóng-jí* (later *jìnshì*) of Jīn Xīng-dìng 5 (1221) at age 32; held office under Zhāng-zōng's last and Āi-zōng's reigns as Magistrate of Chén-liú, Nèi-xiāng, and Nán-yáng, then *Shàng-shū-shěng yuán-wài-láng*, and *Hàn-lín zhī-zhì-gào* / *Zuǒ-sī dū-shì*; was in Kāi-fēng during the Mongol siege; on the dynasty's fall (Tiān-xīng 3 / 1234) he was held briefly captive at Liáo-chéng, then retired to his native Xiù-róng-xiāng 秀容鄉 (in Hé-nán) and to his Yě-shǐ-tíng 野史亭 (Wild-History Pavilion), where for twenty-three years he wrote *bēi*, *zhì*, *zhuàn*, edited *Zhōng-zhōu jí*, and refused to take Yuán service. The collection was assembled and edited posthumously by the Dōng-píng-region Sòng-dynasty defector and Yuán *xíng-tái* governor **Yán Zhōng-jié** 嚴忠傑 (younger brother of the *Dōng-píng-hóu* Yán Shí 嚴實); the principal prefaces by **Lǐ Yě** 李冶 (1262, the great Yuán mathematician of Sì-yuán-shù 四元術 fame, *hào* Fēng-lóng shān-rén) and **Xú Shì-lóng** 徐世隆** plus the postscript by **Wáng È** 王鶚 (1265) — preserved in the SBCK base — are the foundational Yuán-period biographical-and-evaluative documents on Yuán Hǎo-wèn. The collection is organized by genre: *gǔ-fù*; *gǔ-shī*; *zá-yán*; *gǔ yuè-fǔ*; *lǜ-shī* (5- and 7-syllable, occupying *juàn* 7–10 — over 500 pieces, by far the largest body); *jué-jù* (5-, 6-, 7-syllable); *hóng-cí* (one *juàn* of "broad-vocabulary" examination prose); *bēi-míng* / *biǎo* / *zhì* / *jié* (*juàn* 16–31, the great body of Yuán's biographical *zhuàn*-writing for late-Jīn-and-post-conquest literati, monks, Daoist masters, and military men — the principal Yuán-period source for late-Jīn biographical history); *jì* (juàn 32–35); *xù-yǐn* (juàn 36–37, the famous prefaces, including the **Yǐn** to his own anthologies, to friends' collections, and to medical-philosophical books); *míng*, *zàn*, *sòng*; *shū*, *shū*; *zá-tǐ*; *shàng-liáng-wén*, *qīng-cí*; *jì-wén*; *tí-bá*. The 40-*juàn* extent of the present recension is firmly attested in Lǐ Yě's preface ("the whole edition in 40 *juàn* delivered up; in addition the various biographical-pieces, encomia, and presentation-works, I have separately compiled from other collections").

### Tiyao

*No tíyào in the Kanripo source* (the source files reproduce the SBCK base, which preserves the substantial 1262 Lǐ Yě preface, the Xú Shì-lóng preface, the Wáng È *hòu-yǐn* of 1265, and the manuscript letter from Yáng Yún-yì's grandson to Lǐ Xǐng-zhāi about preparing the printing — the principal Yuán-period editorial documents). The Sìkù tíyào outside the source places the *Yí-shān jí* at the head of "Jīn *bié-jí lèi* 4" and characterizes the work as the foundational corpus of Jīn-dynasty literary culture, anchoring three concurrent traditions: (1) Yuán Hǎo-wèn's place as the lineage-master between Zhào Bǐng-wén and the early-Yuán Sòng-Jīn intellectual recovery (Lǐ Yě's preface explicitly establishes the lineage "the senior bearer-of-letters of the past century: in Dà-dìng / Míng-chāng [it was] Hàn-lín Chéng-zhǐ Lord Dǎng; in Zhēn-yòu / Zhèng-dà [it was] Lǐ-bù Lord Zhào; after the Northern Displacement [it is] Yí-shān xiān-shēng, one man only"); (2) Yuán's poetry that "in the seven-syllable verse is sourced in Lǐ-Dù, regulated and fine, with heroic-and-overflowing manner"; "in prose follows Hán-Ōu, broad-and-clear and not in the strange-twisted and obscure language"; "in *yuè-fǔ* is clear-grand and broken-suspended, leisurely-soft and clear-bright, embodying-form most-complete, again able to use the vulgar as elegant — transformed-old-into-new — obtaining the unforgotten subtleties not transmitted by his elders; from Dōng-pō (Sū Shì) and Jiǎ-xuān (Xīn Qì-jí) onward, he is not to be discussed [in their league]"; (3) Yuán's historical-biographical *zhuàn* corpus that "preserves the central-state's literary documents" of the fallen Jīn.

### Abstract

Yuán Hǎo-wèn (CBDB 10074, 1190–1257) is the central literary figure of the Jīn dynasty, the bridge between the late-Jīn cultural florescence and the early-Yuán recovery, and one of the canonical Chinese poets of the second millennium. Native of Xiù-róng-xiāng (Xīn-zhōu 忻州 in Hé-běi-dōng-lù), descended from the Xianbei Tuō-bá clan converted to the surname Yuán by the Northern Wèi. Adopted by his uncle Yuán Gé 元格 (a long-time Jīn local administrator); studied under Hǎo Tiān-tǐng 郝天挺 and was inducted into the *gǔ-wén* tradition through his godfather; recommended to *Hàn-lín* at age 32 by Zhào Bǐng-wén [[KR4d0417]] and subsequently held office through the Mongol siege of Kāi-fēng (1232); was held in the Yán-Shì family compound at Liáo-chéng after the Jīn fall; retired permanently after 1234 to his Xiù-róng estate and the Yě-shǐ-tíng pavilion at his uncle's old grave-site. For the next twenty-three years he composed, on demand, the *bēi-zhì-míng* (epitaph-stele inscriptions) and *xíng-zhuàng* (career biographies) for hundreds of late-Jīn-and-post-conquest figures — preserving in this collection the principal documentary archive of late-Jīn social, religious, military, and intellectual life, on which the Yuán-period Tuō-tuō *Jīn-shǐ* compilers explicitly drew. Concurrently he assembled the *Zhōng-zhōu jí* (anthology of Jīn poetry, 109 figures, with biographical capsules — the principal Jīn-period anthology and one of the great Chinese poetic anthologies) and the *Zhōng-zhōu yuè-fǔ* (*cí*-anthology). Yuán declined all Yuán-state appointments, though Kublai Khan (then *Shì-zǔ*) had earmarked him for the *Tài-shǐ* office.

The poetic corpus is canonical and very large — Yuán's *Lùn-shī jué-jù* 論詩絕句 in 30 pieces (*juàn* 11) is, with Yán Yǔ's *Cāng-làng shī-huà*, the most foundational Sòng-Jīn poetics document; his post-1232 *Jì-yǐn* 紀引 (*juàn* 7's "[Jiāo] *bīng-wū jiā-wū* poems," composed during the siege and surrender of Kāi-fēng), his *Liù-yuè shí-yī rì yǔ* and *Yǐ-yǒu yuè bā yuè-shí qī rì Xīn-zhōu mèng-zhōng suǒ-dé* — these *qì-yùn* preserved direct testimony of the dynastic catastrophe — are among the most-anthologized single Chinese poems. The biographical-stele corpus (juàn 16–31) is the foundational primary source for late-Jīn biography. CBDB 10074 firmly establishes 1190–1257; the *Yuán-shǐ* j. 192 *Rú-xué zhuàn* gives the standard biography. Wilkinson treats Yuán extensively as the principal late-Jīn historian and literary master (§29.1, §28.1, §35).

## Other points of interest

The Yě-shǐ-tíng 野史亭 ("Pavilion of Wild History") at the grave-site of Yuán's uncle in Xiù-róng-xiāng became the site of the principal Yuán-period historiographical activity: there Yuán composed his *Rén-jīn lèi* (Miscellaneous Records of the *Rén-chén* Year, i.e. 1232) and the *Nán-guān lù* 南冠錄 (Record of the Southern Cap, i.e. captive), both manuscript histories of the Jīn dynasty's collapse; both lost as discrete works, but extensively quoted and absorbed by the *Jīn-shǐ* compilers (1343) and preserved in fragments in the present *bié-jí*. The *Lùn-shī jué-jù* — 30 quatrains, each pithy critical commentary on a major Chinese poet from the Han through the Sòng — is the most-cited single poetics document of the Jīn dynasty, anticipating in compressed form the late-Míng / early-Qīng critical-comparative tradition of poetic-judgment quatrains.
