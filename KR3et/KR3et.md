## Scope and scholarly tradition

`KR3et` 法醫醫政類 (*Fǎ-yī yī-zhèng lèi*, Forensic Medicine and Medical Administration) is one of the smallest sub-divisions of `KR3e` 醫家類 (Masters → Medicine). It gathers the two substantive Southern-Sòng handbooks that document the state-institutional, rather than the doctrinal-clinical, face of late-imperial Chinese medicine: the *Xǐyuān jílù* 洗冤集錄 [[KR3et002]] of Sòng Cí, the foundational handbook of the coronial inquest, and the *Tàiyī jú zhūkē chéngwén gé* 太醫局諸科程文格 [[KR3et003]] of Hé Dàrèn, the model-examination compilation of the imperial Medical Bureau. The two works are heterogeneous in subject — judicial autopsy on one hand, medical-school pedagogy on the other — but they share a precisely defined sociological setting: both are operational manuals composed in the early thirteenth century by officials in charge of distinct state apparatuses of Sòng medicine, and printed by those same officials in their bureaucratic capacity (Hé Dàrèn as Director of the *Tàiyī jú* 太醫局 in Lín'ān, 1212–1214; Sòng Cí as circuit *tídiǎn xíngyù* 提點刑獄 of Húnán, 1247). The division thus stands as the *Hǎiwài huíliú* repatriation-series witness to the distinctive Sòng-state institutionalisation of medicine — the system of examined medical specialists in the *Tàiyī jú* and the system of routinely-conducted official inquests under the criminal Code — that is one of the central historiographical novelties of the period. The third entry in the catalog metadata, `KR3et001`, is an empty placeholder titled 易經 with no transmitted content and is not considered here.

As elsewhere in the lettered sub-divisions of `KR3e`, every text in `KR3et` derives from the *Hǎiwài huíliú zhōngyī shànběn gǔjí cóngshū* 海外回流中醫善本古籍叢書 (`source: hxwd`) edited by Zhèng Jīnshēng 鄭金生 et al. (Běijīng: Rénmín wèishēng chūbǎnshè, 2003), the modern repatriation programme that has returned to Chinese-language circulation rare *shànběn* witnesses preserved abroad. The *Tàiyī jú zhūkē chéngwén gé* in this division reproduces a recension descending from the late-Edo Japanese transmission line ([[KR3e0039]] in the parallel WYG mainstream of `KR3e` reproduces the *Sìkù* / Cōngshū jíchéng recension descending from the same returned line). Endymion Wilkinson's *Chinese History: A New Manual* treats both topics: §25.7 (Guides & Handbooks) frames the *Xǐyuān jílù* as the central magistrates'-handbook tradition of late-imperial China alongside the *xíngmíng* 刑名 jurisprudential manuals, while §41 (Medicine) supplies the institutional frame for the *Tàiyī jú* Bureau-examination tradition. The two works are also discussed together in T. J. Hinrichs and Linda L. Barnes, *Chinese Medicine and Healing: An Illustrated History* (Harvard 2013), ch. 4, as paired documentary anchors for "the Sòng state medical apparatus."

## Important texts and text clusters

The division contains only two substantive texts; both are foundational documents of late-Sòng state medicine and do not form a cluster in any meaningful internal sense.

- *Xǐyuān jílù* 洗冤集錄 [[KR3et002]] — Sòng Cí's 1247 five-juan handbook of the coronial inquest, the earliest systematic East-Asian treatise of forensic medicine. Anchored in the statutory framework of the Sòng *Xíng tǒng* 刑統 and in Sòng Cí's own coronial practice as circuit judicial intendant, it became the operational manual of the late-imperial Chinese coroner — through the Qīng *Lǜlìguǎn jiàozhèng Xǐyuān lù* 律例館校證洗冤錄 (1694) — and was transmitted to Korea, Japan and Vietnam as the basis of local inquest practice for some six hundred years. Treated by Wilkinson at §25.7 as one of the central magistrates' handbooks of the imperial era, and by Lu Gwei-djen and Joseph Needham as the high-water mark of premodern East-Asian forensic empiricism. The most consequential single Chinese text on the legal-medical interface.
- *Tàiyī jú zhūkē chéngwén gé* 太醫局諸科程文格 [[KR3et003]] — Hé Dàrèn's 1212–1214 nine-juan compilation of model examination essays for the Southern-Sòng imperial Medical Bureau, organised through the five standard examination genres (*mòyì* 墨義, *màiyì* 脈義, *dàyì* 大義, *jiǎlìng lùn* 假令論, *jiǎlìng fāngyì* 假令方義) across the nine curricular branches of the Bureau (*dàfāngmài* 大方脈, *fēngkē* 風科, *xiǎofāngmài* 小方脈, *yǎnkē* 眼科, *chǎnkē* 產科, *kǒuchǐ jiān yānhóu kē* 口齒兼咽喉科, *chuāngzhǒng jiān zhéyáng kē* 瘡腫兼折瘍科, *zhēnjiǔ kē* 鍼灸科, *jīnzú jiān shūjìn kē* 金鏃兼書禁科). The single most direct surviving witness to actual examination practice in the Sòng state medical school, and thus the principal evidence base for the operative curriculum of the most ambitious Chinese state-medical pedagogy before the modern era. See also the WYG-recension parallel at [[KR3e0039]].

## Important persons

- [[宋慈]] (Sòng Cí, 1186–1249, *zì* Huìfǔ 惠父) — Southern-Sòng *jìnshì* of 1217 and judicial intendant of the Húnán, Jiāngxī, Guǎngdōng and Guǎngxī circuits; sole author of [[KR3et002]] *Xǐyuān jílù*. His preface to that work — moral as well as technical — programmatic insistence on the magistrate-coroner's personal presence at the inquest is the rhetorical core of the late-imperial coronial tradition.
- [[何大任]] (Hé Dàrèn, *fl.* Jiādìng 1208–1224) — Southern-Sòng official-physician with the title *Héān dàfū tèchāi pàn Tàiyī jú* 和安大夫特差判太醫局 (Director of the Imperial Medical Bureau in Lín'ān); compiler and printer of [[KR3et003]] *Tàiyī jú zhūkē chéngwén gé* and of the parallel [[KR3e0031]] *Xiǎo'ér wèishēng zǒngwēi lùn fāng*, both produced as part of his Bureau's coordinated programme of printing authoritative curricular materials for the state medical schools.

## Topics

**Late-Sòng state medicine.** The division's principal historiographical lesson is that the Southern Sòng was the moment at which Chinese medicine was most ambitiously institutionalised by the state — through the *Tàiyī jú* with its nine examined specialties and through the routine coronial inquest under the *tídiǎn xíngyù* — and that the two works in `KR3et` are the operational manuals of those two institutions. This was a moment without sustained continuation: the Yuán dismantled most of the Sòng medical-bureaucratic apparatus, the Míng never restored examined specialty curricula on the Sòng scale, and the routine inquest survived only because the *Xǐyuān* tradition was bureaucratically grafted onto the Qīng Code via the 1694 *Lǜlìguǎn* edition.

**Forensic medicine as a Chinese genre.** The *Xǐyuān jílù* is the founding document of the *fǎyī* 法醫 genre and the principal anchor of the division. Its substantive achievements include systematic procedures for examining hanged, drowned, poisoned, beaten, burned, stabbed and dismembered corpses; the diagnosis of wounds inflicted before versus after death; distinctions between suicide, homicide and accident; the *dīgǔ* 滴骨 bone-blood-drop test for skeletal kinship identification; the use of vinegar, hot wine and heated cloths to bring up suppressed bruising; and an integrated final section on antidotes and resuscitation (*jiùsǐ fāng* 救死方). These observations were rated by Joseph Needham and Lu Gwei-djen as the empirical high-water of premodern East-Asian forensic practice, and the work was the principal locus in which empirical observation about decomposition and trauma circulated within the *zǐbù* corpus.

**The interface between law and medicine.** Both works document the formal points at which the Sòng state required medical expertise from non-physicians (the coronial magistrate) and from physicians required to perform within a legal-bureaucratic frame (the Bureau-examined specialist). The relevant Sòng statutes on the coronial inquest are quoted verbatim in *juàn* 1 of the *Xǐyuān jílù*; the curriculum of the *Tàiyī jú* — including the *jiǎlìng fāngyì* genre that required clinical reasoning under examination conditions — is documented in the *Zhūkē chéngwén gé*. Read together, the two works define the Sòng state's expectation that medicine should be **specified, examinable, and operative** in administrative settings.

**East-Asian transmission.** Both works had long extra-Chinese afterlives: the *Xǐyuān jílù* in Korean (*Sin-ju Mu-wŏn-rok* 新註無冤錄), Japanese and Vietnamese coronial practice from the fourteenth century onward; the *Tàiyī jú zhūkē chéngwén gé* through the bakumatsu *kanpō* / *kǎojù igaku* tradition, which preserved the work after it was lost in mainland China and supplied the recension on which the *Hǎiwài huíliú* return to Chinese-language circulation depends.
