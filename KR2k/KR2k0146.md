# Zhí-fāng wài-jì 職方外紀
*Records Beyond the Domain of the Bureau of Operations* (i.e. of countries beyond the *Zhōu-lǐ* world)
by [[艾儒略]] (Ài Rú-lüè / Giulio Aleni, S.J., 1582–1649) — *zhuàn* 撰

## About the work

A 5-juan late-Míng treatise on world geography in Chinese — the foundational Chinese-language treatment of the Renaissance European geographical worldview — composed by the Italian Jesuit Giulio Aleni 艾儒略 and presented at Bĕi-jīng in *Tiān-qǐ guǐ-hài* (1623). The work draws on Matteo Ricci 利瑪竇's *Wàn-guó tú-zhì* 萬國圖志 (a world map presented to the Wàn-lì emperor) and Diego de Pantoja 龐迪我's translation work, but is substantially Aleni's own composition rather than a polished pre-existing draft. The work treats the entire known globe under the European late-Renaissance scheme of five continents — *Yà-xì-yà* (Asia), *Ōu-luó-bā* (Europe), *Lì-wèi-yà* (Africa), *Yà-mò-lì-jiā* (the Americas, divided North and South), and *Mò-wǎ-là-ní-jiā* (Magellanica = the southern unknown continent then theorised in European cosmography) — providing for each continent its boundaries, principal countries, customs, and notable cities. Prefaced by a *Wàn-guó quán-tú* (full world map) and concluded with a *Sì-hǎi zǒng-shuō* (general account of the four oceans). The work is the most important pre-Qīng Chinese-language exposition of European world geography and the foundation of subsequent Chinese world-geographical literature (including Náng Huái-rén [Verbiest]'s *Kūn-yú tú-shuō* [[KR2k0150]] of 1672).

### Tiyao

We respectfully note: the *Zhí-fāng wài-jì* in five juan is by the Western scholar Ài Rú-lüè of Míng. The work was completed in *Tiān-qǐ guǐ-hài* (1623); on the basis of the *Wàn-guó tú-zhì* presented by the Westerner Lì [Mǎ-dòu] (Matteo Ricci), and on Páng [Dí-wǒ]'s (Diego de Pantoja's) imperial-commissioned translation, Rú-lüè further supplemented and revised it; presumably built on Lì Mǎ-dòu and Páng Dí-wǒ's old text, embellished — but not entirely Rú-lüè's own composition.

What is recorded is all the customs of remote regions, what the *yú-tú* of antiquity has not recorded — hence titled *Zhí-fāng wài-jì*. Its treatment divides the *tiān-xià* into five great continents:

(1) *Yà-xì-yà*: from the west at *Nà-duō-lǐ-yà* (Anatolia), 62° from the *Fú-dǎo* (Fortunate Islands / Canary Islands prime meridian); to the east at *Yà-ní-yān xiá* (Strait of Anián = the supposed strait between America and Asia), 180°; from the south at *Guā-wā* (Java), 12° south of the equator; to the north at *Bīng-hǎi* (the Arctic), 72° north.

(2) *Ōu-luó-bā*: from the south at the Mediterranean, 35° N; to the north at the Arctic, 80°+; spanning 11,250 *lǐ*; from the west at the Western Sea Fortunate Islands at 0°; to the east at the *Ā-běi* River (Ob), 92° from the Fortunate Islands; spanning 23,000 *lǐ*.

(3) *Lì-wèi-yà* (Africa): southwest all to the *Lì-wèi-yà-hǎi* (Atlantic); east to the *Xī Hóng-hǎi* (Red Sea); north to the Mediterranean; extreme south 35° S; extreme north 35° N; east-west 78°.

(4) *Yà-mò-lì-jiā*: divided North-and-South, with a connecting isthmus; south of the isthmus from *Mò-wǎ-là-ní hǎi-xiá* (Strait of Magellan), 52° S, north to *Jiā-nà-dá*, 10.5° N; west from 286°, east to 355°. North of the isthmus from *Jiā-nà-dá* 10.5° N to the Arctic — the latitude not measured; west from 180°, east to the Fortunate Islands at 360°.

(5) *Mò-wǎ-là-ní-jiā* (Magellanica) — recently in contact with that country [Spain]; territorial extent and distances not yet ascertained.

Prefaced by the *Wàn-guó quán-tú* and appended with the *Sì-hǎi zǒng-shuō*. What is described is mostly strange-and-unusual, beyond exhaustive verification — seems unavoidable to be much exaggerated; but the great-and-small of heaven-and-earth, what is there that does not exist? Recording and preserving it is also enough to broaden seeing-and-hearing.

### Abstract

The *Zhí-fāng wài-jì* is the most important pre-Qīng Chinese-language exposition of European world geography. It was composed by the Italian Jesuit missionary Giulio Aleni 艾儒略 (1582–1649; in Chinese 艾思及 *zì* Sī-jí), one of the most distinguished members of the late-Míng Jesuit China mission, the first European to enter the inner imperial precincts of Fú-jiàn (where he established the Mǐn-zhōng mission), and a major writer in the China-Jesuit syncretic-philosophical tradition. The work was presented at Běi-jīng in *Tiān-qǐ* 3 *guǐ-hài* (1623) on the basis of inherited material from Matteo Ricci's *Wàn-guó tú-zhì* (the world maps presented to Wàn-lì in 1602 and later revised) and Diego de Pantoja's translation work, but substantially in Aleni's own composition.

The work's principal scholarly value is in being the first Chinese-language treatment of: (i) the Aristotelian-Ptolemaic-Renaissance scheme of a spherical earth divided into five continents (Asia / Europe / Africa / Americas / Magellanica); (ii) European world cartography with the prime meridian at the Canary Islands ("*Fú-dǎo*"); (iii) latitude-and-longitude as the standard Chinese cosmographical reference (replacing the *Yǔ-gòng* nine-province scheme); (iv) detailed accounts of Renaissance European cities and institutions (Rome, Venice, Florence, Paris, Madrid, the Holy Roman Empire); (v) the Americas — Aleni's account is the first sustained Chinese-language treatment of the New World; (vi) the Spanish and Portuguese maritime empires.

The Sìkù compilers, working a century-and-a-half later, retained the work in the *wài-jì* category despite their evident skepticism about much of its content — preserving it as a curiosity ("for broadening seeing-and-hearing"). The work was nevertheless one of the principal early-Qīng sources for Chinese knowledge of the European world, and is foundational for the late-Qīng "Western learning" (*Xī-xué*) tradition.

The work is preserved in *Wényuāngé Sìkù quánshū* (vol. 594.8).

## Other points of interest

The work is the principal Chinese channel through which the Renaissance-cosmographical worldview entered late-imperial Chinese intellectual culture, and is regularly cited in late-Qīng *Yáng-wù* (Western affairs) discussions. The Sìkù compilers' decision to retain it under their dìlǐ rubric — despite their skeptical stance — is itself an interesting marker of the gradual Qīng-period absorption of European geographical knowledge.
