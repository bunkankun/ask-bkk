# Bǎo Yuè lù 保越錄
*Record of the Defense of Yuè (Shàoxīng)*
by anonymous (闕名 撰)

## About the work
A 1-*juàn* eyewitness account of the three-month siege of *Shàoxīng* 紹興 (called *Yuè-zhōu* 越州 by its old name) in 1359 by Hú Dà-hǎi's 胡大海 forces of Zhū Yuán-zhāng 朱元璋, repelled by the Zhāng Shì-chéng 張士誠 garrison commander Lǚ Zhēn 吕珍. The narrative runs from late *Zhì-zhèng* 18 (1358 winter, when Lǚ Zhēn took up command after the murder of *Mù-ěr-gǔ-sū* 穆爾古蘇) through the three-month siege of *Zhì-zhèng* 19 (spring–summer 1359), and through the battles' resolution. The siege was one of the most consequential operations of the late-Yuán conflict between Zhū Yuán-zhāng's *Wú-Hàn* faction and Zhāng Shì-chéng's *Wú* faction; Lǚ Zhēn's successful defense bought *Shàoxīng* eight more years of independence under the Zhāng (until 1366). The compiler's name has been lost; from the text's perspective — sympathetic to Lǚ Zhēn, calling Zhāng's troops "*wǒ jūn*" (our army) and Hú Dà-hǎi's *Wú-Hàn* forces "*Dà-jūn*" (the Great Army, with reverent later-period substitution) — the *Sì-kù* editors deduced that the original was composed by a *Shàoxīng* resident during the still-extant Zhāng-shì-chéng polity, with the references to *Tài-zǔ Gāo Huáng-dì* and *Dà-jūn* being later Míng emendations.

The text is preserved in the *Sì-kù quán-shū* with several lacunae (marked 闕 in the original), reflecting damage in the only surviving recension. It is dense with personal names and military detail: Lǚ Zhēn's twelve gate-commanders and their deputies; the layered defensive works (palisade-and-moat, then *yuè-chéng* 月城 / barbican with watch-tower and crenellated parapets, then earthworks beyond); the daily-by-daily attack-and-counter-attack record; the famine-relief logistics (the rice-and-salt exchange with Hángzhōu; the inland-water supply convoy from Jiā-xīng 嘉興 via the East China Sea); the conduct of war (Lǚ's repeated leniency in releasing captured *Wú-Hàn* prisoners, his strict suppression of city-panic, his refusal to over-pursue the retreating enemy at the end). Striking moments: the suicide-pact of the gentry-scholar Zhāng Zhèng-méng's 張正䝉 wife Hán-shì 韓氏 (daughter of the local Confucian Hán Xìng 韓性) and her two daughters, refusing capture; the encounter between the rebel-painter Wáng Miǎn 王冕 (元章, the famous plum-blossom painter) — who had refused to seek shelter inside the city — and Hú Dà-hǎi, with Wáng Miǎn subsequently advising the *Wú-Hàn* forces on a deceptive flank attack at *Yòu-yàn* 右堰; and the climactic divine-wind that scattered Hú Dà-hǎi's fire-ships and broke the assault on the third month *bǐng-wǔ*.

### Tiyao
*Bǎo Yuè lù* in 1 *juàn*, author's name not preserved. Records the events of *Zhì-zhèng* 19 (1359), when the Míng forces attacked Shàoxīng. At that time the Míng commander was Hú Dà-hǎi; the defender was Zhāng Shì-chéng's general Lǚ Zhēn. They besieged for three months but ultimately could not take it, and so withdrew. This *lù* refers to Shì-chéng's troops as "*wǒ jūn*" (our army) and to Lǚ Zhēn as "*gōng*" (the lord) — apparently a *Shàoxīng* resident's record while Shì-chéng's polity was still extant. The references to "*Míng*" as "*Dà-jūn*" and to "*Tài-zǔ Gāo-huáng-dì*" — these are perhaps later Míng-period transcribers' alterations after Shì-chéng's fall. Shàoxīng from this point held its own for eight more years; in *Zhì-zhèng* 26 (1366) it was finally returned to Míng. Lǚ Zhēn likewise in that year, after the Húzhōu defeat, surrendered to Xú Dá. Although his initial allegiance was misplaced and his late integrity faltered, in respect to Shàoxīng he cannot be said to have rendered no service. As for Hú Dà-hǎi's repulse at Shàoxīng, his subsequent licentious looting and disturbance of Sòng tomb-mounds and other deeds of cruelty — the *Míng shǐ* records none of them. The recorded events of Zhāng Zhèng-méng's wife Hán-shì, his daughters Chí-nú and Yuè-nú, and the wives of Féng Dào-yī and Féng Dào-èr resisting and dying for principle — the *Míng shǐ* likewise does not record. These especially supplement the omissions of the standard history; therefore we record this work for reference. Reverently presented in the sixth month of *Qiánlóng* 45 (= 1780). Chief Editors: Jì Yún, Lù Xī-xióng, Sūn Shì-yì. Chief Collator: Lù Fèi-chí.

### Abstract
The *Bǎo Yuè lù* is the principal Yuán-end / late Yuán-Míng-transitional eyewitness source on the conflict between Zhū Yuán-zhāng and Zhāng Shì-chéng. Its specific focus — the siege of *Shàoxīng* in 1359 — is one of the few episodes of that conflict for which a contemporary partisan record survives. The work's composition is firmly bracketed by internal evidence: the events occur 1358–59; the work was composed during the surviving Zhāng-shì-chéng polity (i.e. before 1366, when *Shàoxīng* surrendered); and almost certainly while Lǚ Zhēn was still in command at *Shàoxīng* (i.e., before his redeployment to Húzhōu in 1366) — yielding a defensible composition window of 1359–1367.

The work's interest is both historiographical and literary: (i) as an unflinching record of the siege and counter-siege tactics of mid-fourteenth-century Chinese warfare — moats and palisades, *yuè-chéng* (barbican) construction, fire-arrows, fire-tubes (*huǒ-tǒng* 火筒), siege-towers, fascine-and-bundle bridges across moats, and counter-mining; (ii) as a corrective to the official *Míng shǐ*, which (the *Sì-kù* editors point out) systematically suppresses Hú Dà-hǎi's failure at Shàoxīng, his troops' looting and grave-desecration (specifically of the Southern-Sòng *Lǐ-zōng* and *Cí-xiàn fū-rén* tombs), and the resistance of *Shàoxīng* gentry-women — items the *Míng shǐ* cannot record because they reflect badly on the Míng founder; (iii) as a uniquely vivid social-political portrait of late-Yuán urban resistance, including the Confucian gentry's collective response (suicide-pacts, civilian organization in *bǎo-jiǎ* 保甲 units), the food-and-supply networks linking Shàoxīng to Hángzhōu and Jiā-xīng, and the role of the painter-rebel Wáng Miǎn 王冕 as a *jūn-shī* (military advisor) to Hú Dà-hǎi.

The text is also one of the few sources documenting the late-Yuán polity's continued resilience: under Lǚ Zhēn's command Shàoxīng held for eight more years, until 1366 — proving that the *Wú-Hàn* / Zhū-Yuán-zhāng victory was neither swift nor inevitable but the result of a long protracted struggle.

## Other points of interest
The Wáng Miǎn episode is striking: the famous late-Yuán plum-blossom painter, recorded as having taken refuge in the *Jiǔ-lǐ shān* 九里山 outside Shàoxīng and refusing to enter the city, is captured by Hú Dà-hǎi's troops; he saves his life by claiming military expertise (*shàn-néng tāo-lüè bīng-shū* — well-versed in military *tāo-lüè* and military classics); is presented to Zhū Yuán-zhāng, who promotes him to a high command position; and serves as a *jūn-shī* to Hú Dà-hǎi for the Shàoxīng campaign. He is credited with proposing the deceptive flank attack at the *Yòu-yàn* (Right Embankment) — a manoeuvre that nearly succeeded in cutting Shàoxīng's grain route through *Chāng-ān-mén*. The *Bǎo Yuè lù*'s testimony stands in sharp contrast to the romanticized later Míng-Qīng image of Wáng Miǎn as a pure-hearted recluse-painter; a useful corrective to Wáng Miǎn's biography in standard reference works.
