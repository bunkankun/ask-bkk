# Héng-chuān Xíng-gǒng chánshī yǔlù 橫川行珙禪師語錄

Two-juan *yǔlù* of **Héng-chuān Rú-gǒng / Xíng-gǒng** [[如珙]] 橫川如珙 / 橫川行珙 (*zì* Zǐ-pú 子璞; 1222 – 18 March 1289), Yángqí-branch Línjì master of the late Southern Sòng / early Yuán transition, compiled by his disciple [[本光]] Běn-guāng, abbot of Yàndàng-shān Luóhàn chán-sì 鴈蕩山羅漢禪寺. The CBETA Xuzangjing edition (X71 no. 1411) carries the name 行珙 on its title-slip, but the standard form in Yuán and later Chinese sources is 如珙 — the DILA person authority lists both as names of the same person (A007633). The text organizes itself by three abbacies, each giving its name to a "record" in *juan* 1: the Líng-yán *lù* 靈巖錄 (from his first installation at Ruì-ān-fǔ Yàndàng-shān Líng-yán chán-sì 瑞安府鴈蕩山靈巖禪寺 on the 28th of the 9th month of Xiánchún 4 / 1268), the Néng-rén *lù* 能仁錄 (from Xiánchún 8 / 1272), and the Yù-wáng *lù* 育王錄 (from his imperial appointment to Míng-zhōu Āyù-wáng-shān Guǎng-lì chán-sì in Zhìyuán 16 / 1279). *Juan* 2 gathers *shì-zhōng jǔ-gǔ* 室中舉古, *niān-gǔ*, *sòng-gǔ*, *zàn*, and a long sequence of *jì-sòng* — closing with a self-composed *tǎ-jì* and *tǎ-míng* ("病叟今年六十六…") written in his 66th year (1287) and identifying Gǔ-mào-shān 古鄮山 (the temple-mountain of Āyù-wáng) as his chosen burial site.

### Abstract

The collection is framed by the master's own short self-preface dated the winter of Wùzǐ (1288) — placed only months before his death on the 18th of the 3rd month of Zhìyuán 26 (16 April 1289) — which describes it as gathered by "countless disciples" 河沙數禪人 集余語, a formula reinforced by Běn-guāng's editorial byline on *juan* 1. Rú-gǒng was a native of Yǒngjiā 永嘉 (Zhèjiāng), lay surname Lín 林; he was tonsured at fifteen by his paternal uncle, the monk Zhèng-zé 正則, and trained early at Líng-yǐn under Shí-tián Fǎ-xūn 石田法薰 and Chī-jué Dào-chōng 癡絕道冲 before finding his teacher Tiān-mù Wén-lǐ 天目文禮 (1167–1250) at Tàibǎi-shān (Tiān-tóng), where Wén-lǐ was abbot as the "9th Yángqí generation." Rú-gǒng's opening incense-lighting at Líng-yán offered formal thanks to Wén-lǐ — "Dū-huáng-yá 督黃牙 at Tàibǎi-shān, citing the Nán-shān bamboo-shoots and East Sea cuttlefish," — and set the terms of his abbacy's engagement with the *Bì-yán lù* tradition in Jiāngnán. His first installation in 1268 was at the request of a *píng-zhāng guó-gōng* 平章國公 (the *tǎ-jì* in the *Mù-qián jí* 牧潛集 *juan* 3 identifies the patron); his second at Néng-rén came from the same Southern-Sòng court network; his final, Āyù-wáng appointment came by Yuán imperial edict in 1279, two years after the Mongol takeover of Jiāng-nán, and made him one of the first old Sòng-trained Chán masters to occupy a major coastal monastery under the new regime. The *Bǔ-xù gāo-sēng zhuàn* 補續高僧傳 *juan* 12 and *Zēng-jí Xù-chuándēng-lù* *juan* 4 preserve the principal biographical notices; the *Mù-qián jí* *tǎ-jì* is the earliest and most authoritative source. Named dharma-heirs include the three pillars of early-Yuán Jiāng-nán Chán: [[妙道]] Zhú-yuán Miào-dào 竺原妙道, [[清茂]] Gǔ-lín Qīng-mào 古林清茂 (see [[KR6q0345]], [[KR6q0346]]), and [[覺恩]] Duàn-jiāng Jué-ēn 斷江覺恩; through them — and especially through Qīng-mào's extensive Japanese following and through Qīng-mào's and Miào-dào's continued transmissions into the Yuán hierarchy — Rú-gǒng's line became the dominant strand of late-Sòng-into-Yuán Yángqí orthodoxy in Zhèjiāng and abroad.

## Translations and research

*No substantial secondary literature located* in Western-language scholarship. Chinese and Japanese coverage concentrates on his lineage (especially via Qīng-mào) in histories of Zen-Buddhist transmission to Japan, where several of his dharma-grandchildren through Qīng-mào — among them Yuán-dynasty monks received at Japanese temples — carried his teaching forward; a partial Japanese secondary literature therefore attaches to him indirectly through Wǔ-shān bungaku 五山文學 / Wǔ-shān Chán transmission studies. The *Mù-qián jí* *tǎ-jì* by Nǎi-xián 迺賢 / his circle is the standard biographical source in Chinese.

## Other points of interest

The self-composed closing *tǎ-míng* — "Heaven produces one cavity, / it will hold my withered bones; / bones rot and turn to earth, / and the earth can generate things; / I shape a gourd / and hang it on Zhào-zhōu's wall…" — is an unusual case of a Chán master drafting his own stupa inscription within his *yǔlù*, and was later cited in the *Mù-qián jí* *tǎ-jì* as authority for the actual stupa at Gǔ-mào-shān.
