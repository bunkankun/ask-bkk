# Rìjiǎng Sìshū jiěyì 日講四書解義
*Imperial Daily-Lectures: Explication of the Four Books*

by [[喇沙里]] (Lǎshālǐ / Lasari, fl. early Kāngxī, 奉敕編), [[陳廷敬]] (Chén Tíng-jìng, 1639–1712, 奉敕編), with the Hànlín lecturer-academician group of [[庫勒納]] (Kù-lè-nà), [[葉方藹]] (Yè Fāng-ǎi), [[張英]] (Zhāng Yīng) and others.

## About the work

The *Rìjiǎng Sì-shū jiě-yì* in 26 *juàn* is the official Qīng imperial-court *Sì-shū* exposition: it preserves the daily lectures (*rì-jiǎng*) delivered before the young Kāngxī emperor by his Hànlín lecturer-academicians at the Hóngdé-diàn 弘德殿. Compilation was completed in 1677 (Kāngxī 16) and the work was published with the emperor's *yù-zhì* preface dated the twelfth month of that year. It is not a fresh exegesis but an authorised *Cheng-Zhu*-orthodox reading channelled into a court-lecture form, with Zhū Xī's *Sì-shū jí-zhù* 四書集注 supplying the controlling framework throughout. The work covers all four books — *Dà-xué*, *Lúnyǔ*, *Mèngzǐ*, *Zhōng-yōng* — under one continuous numbering. As an imperial imprimatur on Cheng-Zhu *Sì-shū* learning at the start of the long Kāngxī reign, it became the de facto official-orthodox commentary set against which later Qīng *Sì-shū* scholarship had to position itself.

### Tiyao

(*tíyào* not present in the WYG front matter; supplied here from the Kyoto Zinbun digital *Sìkù tíyào*, *Jīng-bù 36, Sì-shū lèi èr*, no. 0074602):

We respectfully submit: *Rì-jiǎng Sì-shū jiě-yì* in twenty-six *juàn* — imperially fixed by the Sage-zǔ Rén-huáng-dì in the sixteenth year of Kāngxī. From the time Zhū-zǐ established the *Sì-shū*, through the Yuán and Míng down to the present dynasty, it has been hung up as the standard for the examinations: every household chants it, every door recites it — almost to the point that it is taken as familiar and unremarkable. In reality, the way of the *nèi-shèng wài-wáng* 內聖外王 (sage-within-and-king-without) is fully present in Confucius; the heart-method (*xīn-fǎ*) of Confucius is lodged in the *Six Classics*; the kernel of the *Six Classics* is gathered in the *Lúnyǔ*; while Zēng-zǐ, Zǐ-sī, and Mèngzǐ pass on its thread in succession. Hence the *Lúnyǔ* begins with the discourse on learning and ends with the governance of Yáo, Shùn, Tāng, and Wǔ — instruction in honouring goodness and rejecting evil; the *Dà-xué* begins with *gé-wù zhì-zhī* (investigation of things and extension of knowing) and ends with ordering the state and pacifying-the-realm; the *Zhōng-yōng* begins with *zhōng-hé wèi-yù* and ends with reverent humility producing the peace of the realm; the *Mèngzǐ* begins with the *yì-lì* distinction and ends with the *dào-tǒng* descended from Yáo and Shùn. The *dà-zhǐ* of the sage-and-worthies' establishing words is plainly discernible — it is the pivot of the imperial way through the ages, not merely the sectional-and-phrasal business of jīng-shēng. Our Sage-zǔ Rén-huáng-dì, in his early-reign *fǎng-luò* (court-consultation period), in person fixed this compilation by way of *jīng-yán* lecture-articles. What is unfolded therein is the basis of becoming-a-sage and the root of governance: the diction is near, but the meaning is distant; the language is concise, but the way is vast. The sagely virtue and divine merit by which the transmission of Zhū-Sì 洙泗 is touched and the track of Táng-Yú resumed — all begin from this. (*Sìkù tíyào*, abbreviated, Zinbun ID 0074602.)

### Abstract

The *Rì-jiǎng Sì-shū jiě-yì* is the principal product of the early-Kāngxī *jīng-yán rì-jiǎng* (classics-mat daily-lectures) institution, by which the young emperor — having taken the throne in 1661 at age seven and shaken off the regent Áo-bài 鰲拜 in 1669 — was tutored daily on the *Sì-shū* and *Wǔ-jīng* by a rotating group of Hànlín academicians at the Hóngdé-diàn. The completed *Sì-shū* lectures were submitted to the emperor in the twelfth month of Kāngxī 16 (1677), with a 進呈疏 (memorial of submission) signed jointly by Lǎshālǐ, Chén Tíng-jìng, Sè-lěng, Yè Fāng-ǎi, Jiǎng Hóng-dào, Kù-lè-nà, and Zhāng Yīng; Kù-lè-nà and Yè Fāng-ǎi are named as 總裁官 (chief editors), with a long list of *fēn-zhuàn-guān* (apportioning compilers) led by Gé-ěr-gǔ-dé 格爾古德 attached at the back. The emperor's own *yù-zhì xù* dated 1677/12/8 frames the work as a public *huà-mín chéng-sú* 化民成俗 instrument, using the lecture series as the means of "rectifying the heart and clarifying learning" for the realm.

The compilation method is direct exposition of the *Sì-shū jí-zhù* of Zhū Xī, paragraph by paragraph, with each lecture-day covering a section. The orthodoxy is openly Cheng-Zhu; there is no trace of Yáng-míng *Xīn-xué*, no admixture of *Hàn-xué* philological glossing, and no engagement with the late-Míng *liáng-zhī* tradition. As the first major *Sì-shū* publication of the new dynasty's mature classical-learning programme — and the one bearing the emperor's personal *yù-zhì* preface — it set the template for the *Rì-jiǎng* series, parallels of which were produced for the *Yì*, *Shū*, *Shī*, *Chūnqiū*, and *Lǐ-jì* over the following two decades.

The dating is fixed exactly: the work is *post quem* the start of the Kāngxī *rì-jiǎng* programme (1670, Kāngxī 9, when daily lectures were instituted) and *ad quem* the 1677 submission. The Sìkù catalogues it under the *Sì-shū lèi èr* of *jīng-bù*, and the *tíyào* (preserved in the Zinbun digital edition rather than the WYG front matter, which carries only the imperial preface and the submission-memorial) frames it as the public re-establishment of the *Zhū-Sì dào-tǒng* by the new Manchu emperor.

## Translations and research

No English translation. Modern Chinese: 識典古籍 hosts a punctuated digital edition; the *Wényuān-gé Sì-kù-quán-shū* photo-reprint is the standard scholarly text. Studies: On the *jīng-yán rì-jiǎng* institution and the textual production around the young Kāngxī, see Jonathan Spence, *Emperor of China: Self-Portrait of K'ang-hsi* (Knopf, 1974) and the relevant chapters of R. Kent Guy, *The Emperor's Four Treasuries* (Harvard, 1987); on Kāngxī's classical-learning programme more broadly, On-cho Ng, *Cheng-Zhu Confucianism in the Early Qing* (SUNY, 2001), and Pei-yi Wu's discussion in *The Confucian's Progress* (Princeton, 1990). For the place of Cheng-Zhu *Sì-shū* learning in early-Qīng court ideology, Daniel Gardner, *Zhu Xi's Reading of the Analects* (Columbia, 2003), is the principal English study of the controlling commentary.

## Other points of interest

The work is *the* principal example of the *yù-zhuàn* / *yù-dìng* genre of *Sì-shū* exegesis — Sì-shū learning produced under direct imperial sponsorship, taking shape inside the *jīng-yán rì-jiǎng* institution rather than in private scholarly circles. It is in this respect the Qīng counterpart of the Yǒng-lè-era *Sì-shū dà-quán* 四書大全 (Míng), but with one major difference: the *Dà-quán* is a synthetic *bian-zuǎn* of prior commentary, whereas the *Rì-jiǎng* is a freshly-written court lecture series, oral in origin and only secondarily printed. The 進呈疏 explicitly invokes the precedent of Zhào Pǔ's "half a *Lúnyǔ* governs the empire" and Zhū Xī's "four characters" *chéng-yì zhèng-xīn* — situating the imperial *Sì-shū* lecture within a continuous Cheng-Zhu *jīng-yán* tradition.
