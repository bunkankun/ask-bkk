# Gāoshàng Yùhuáng běnxíng jíjīng (yī) 高上玉皇本行集經(一)

*Collected Scripture of the Original Conduct of the Supreme Jade Emperor, Part 1 (Běnwén 本文 Scripture)*

anonymous late-Southern-Sòng Qīngwēi-school 清微 scripture of the Jade Emperor cult, three juan in five sections (品), the *běnwén* 本文 "fundamental scripture" of the *Yùhuáng* 玉皇 complex preserved in the *Zhèngtǒng Dàozàng* 正統道藏 (DZ 0010 / CT 10), 洞真部 本文類; paired in the Daozang with [[KR5a0011|DZ 11]] (the attributed-to-Zhāng-Liáng commentary) and in the *Dàozàng jíyào* 道藏輯要 combined as JY088

## About the work

A three-juan five-section scripture on the origin and transformative activity of Yùhuáng 玉皇 ("The Jade Emperor"), the supreme deity of popular and late-imperial Daoism. The scripture is set in the Qīngwēi 清微 heaven — the highest of the Three Pure Realms and the seat of the Qīngwēi-school pantheon — where Yuánshǐ tiānzūn 元始天尊 preaches the "pure, untainted, non-dual doctrine" (清淨真一不二法門) to an assembly including Yùhuáng himself. Yùhuáng then multiplies his body to address all regions of the cosmos, recombines, and narrates his own cosmic biography: his origin as an avatar of the Most High Dàojūn 道君, his 3,200 *kalpa* cultivation, his ascent to the rank of Golden Immortal (金仙) under the Mahāyāna title Rúlái 如來 ("King of Pure and Spontaneous Awakening," 淨覺自然王如來), and his consequent teaching of the true *dàshèng* 大乘 doctrine to the host of bodhisattvas. The text concludes with an enumeration of the thirty wondrous merits attendant upon reverent recitation — including the destruction of the *Tiěwéi* 鐵圍 iron-ringed hells, the abolition of the sea of suffering, the breaking of the great prisons, the subjugation of evil *mó* 魔 demons, and thirty blessings ranging from "praise by the host of immortals" to "ascent in broad daylight."

Structurally the three juan are divided into five *pǐn*: 清微天宮神通品 ("The Miracles of the Qīngwēi Heavenly Palace"), the scripture's opening and revelation-scene, and four further sections treating the Jade Emperor's past lives, cosmic preaching, salvational power, and the merits of recitation. Juan 2 contains an embedded revelation of the *Língbǎo chìshū yùpiān zhēnwén* 靈寶赤書玉篇真文 ("True Writs in Vermilion Writing on Jade Tablets") drawn directly from [[KR5a0022|DZ 22 *Yuánshǐ wǔlǎo chìshū yùpiān zhēnwén tiānshū jīng* 元始五老赤書玉篇真文天書經]], integrating the Jade Emperor cult textually with the early Língbǎo corpus.

### Prefaces

*No prefaces in the source.* The received Daozang recension preserves the scripture alone, without the 1240 Hangzhou imprint-preface by Chéng Gōngxǔ 程公許 (*jìnshì* 1211) recorded in the *Jùnzhāi dúshū zhì* 郡齋讀書志 "Fùzhì" 附志 (1249, at entry 1161). The Chéng Gōngxǔ preface would have given the direct textual witness for the Sichuan-origin ( *Shǔběn* 蜀本) text-history, but is lost. Each juan opens directly with the revelation frame.

### Abstract

The *Yùhuáng běnxíng jíjīng* was not in circulation before the thirteenth century. The earliest documentary anchor is the bibliographical entry in the Fùzhì 附志 (1249) supplement to Cháo Gōngwǔ's 晁公武 *Jùnzhāi dúshū zhì* 郡齋讀書志 (item 1161), which records an edition printed in 1240 by the Chéngtiān língyìng guàn 承天靈應觀 in Hángzhōu, itself based on a Sichuan recension (蜀本), with a preface by Chéng Gōngxǔ 程公許 (*jìnshì* 1211). The Chéngtiān língyìng guàn is recorded in *Xiánchún Lín'ān zhì* 咸淳臨安志 75.8a; it was a cult-centre dedicated to Zǐtóng dìjūn 梓潼帝君 (i.e. Wénchāng 文昌) and received its name in 1236. Quotations of the scripture appear in a number of Yuán-era Daoist works, notably DZ 103 *Yùqīng wújí zǒngzhēn Wénchāng dàdòng xiānjīng zhù* 5.17a, 25b (Wèi Qí, 1310 — the commentary on [[KR5a0005|DZ 5]]).

Kristofer Schipper's entry in *The Taoist Canon* (2004) 2:1097, §3.B.7 ("The Qingwei School"), identifies the *Yùhuáng jīng* as a scripture of the Qīngwēi 清微 school — the late-Sòng / Yuán Daoist movement that treated itself as successor to the Shénxiāo 神霄 and developed Thunder-rite (*léifǎ* 雷法) liturgies. Although the *Yùhuáng jīng* itself does not mention the Qīngwēi school by name, the related *Gāoshàng Yùhuáng mǎnyuàn bǎozhàn* 高上玉皇滿願寳懺 (DZ 194) explicitly invokes the Qīngwēi patriarchs, securing the affiliation. The extent to which the *Yùhuáng jīng* may be a reconstitution of the lost early-Língbǎo *Língbǎo zhēnwén dùrén běnxíng miàojīng* 靈寳真文度人本行妙經 (Lagerwey, *Wu-shang pi-yao*, p. 265) is difficult to determine.

The frontmatter brackets the composition notBefore 1180 (latest plausible late-Southern-Sòng window, consistent with both the undated textual anchor and the Qīngwēi-school chronology) / notAfter 1240 (the Hangzhou printing), with dynasty 南宋. The catalog meta lists no attributed author for DZ 10; *persons* is accordingly empty. (The DZ 11 commentary is spuriously attributed to Zhāng Liáng 張良, d. 187 BCE — see [[KR5a0011]].)

## Translations and research

No complete translation exists. Standard scholarly entry: Kristofer Schipper, "Gaoshang yuhuang benxing jijing," in Schipper & Verellen eds., *The Taoist Canon* (2004), Vol. 2, §3.B.7, 1096–1097 (the school introduction) and 1097 (the DZ 10 entry). The wider Jade-Emperor cult has received monographic treatment in: Paul R. Katz, *Religion in China and Its Modern Fate* (Brandeis University Press, 2014), with substantial earlier work in his *Demon Hordes and Burning Boats: The Cult of Marshal Wen in Late Imperial Chekiang* (SUNY Press, 1995); Lennert Gesterkamp, *The Heavenly Court: Daoist Temple Painting in China, 1200–1400* (Brill, 2011), for the Jade Emperor in iconography. Matsumoto Kōichi 松本浩一, *Sōdai no dōkyō to minkan shinkō* 宋代の道教と民間信仰 (Kyūko Shoin, 2006), treats the Sòng-dynasty formation of the cult.

The Qīngwēi school's own scriptural and ritual corpus, including the *Yùhuáng jīng*, is treated in Lǔ Guólóng 盧國龍, *Zhōngguó zhòngxuán xué* 中國重玄學 (Rénmín Zhōngguó chūbǎnshè, 1993), and in the entries of the *Zhōnghuá Dàojiào dà cídiǎn* 中華道教大辭典 (Zhōngguó shèhuì kēxué chūbǎnshè, 1995).

## Other points of interest

The *Yùhuáng běnxíng jíjīng* is the most-recited scripture of the Jade Emperor cult through the Yuán, Míng, and Qīng and into the twentieth century; modern popular Daoist temple editions of the text — the *Yùhuáng jīng* 玉皇經 — descend directly from this Sòng recension. The text's placement at DZ 10 in the *Zhèngtǒng Dàozàng* reflects its canonical importance in the Míng editorial conception: immediately after the core Shàngqīng and Língbǎo scriptures (DZ 1–9) stands the scripture of the Jade Emperor as supreme deity of popular worship — a placement that consolidated, editorially, the Jade Emperor's displacement of more archaic deities from the centre of the canon.
