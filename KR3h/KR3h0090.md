# Yuán-yě 園冶
*The Craft of Gardens*
by [[計成]] (Jì Chéng, b. 1582, fl. 1631–1635, 明, *zhuàn* 撰)

## About the work

The single most important Chinese theoretical treatise on garden design, composed by Jì Chéng 計成 (*zì* Wú-pǐ 無否, *hào* Pǐ-dào rén 否道人, of Wú-jiāng 吳江 in Sūzhōu prefecture, b. 1582). The work was completed in *Chóng-zhēn xīn-wèi* 崇禎辛未 (1631) and published in *Chóng-zhēn jiǎ-xū* 崇禎甲戌 (1634) with a postface by Jì then aged 53. Originally titled *Yuán-mù* 園牧 (the "Garden Pastorate"), the title was changed to *Yuán-yě* 園冶 ("Garden Crafting") at the suggestion of Cáo Yuán-fǔ 曹元甫 of Gū-shú 姑孰 (i.e. Dāng-tú 當塗). The treatise is in three *juàn*: *juàn* 1 covers (1) *Xìng-zào lùn* 興造論 (theory of construction), and the *Xiāng-dì* 相地 (site-divining), *Lì-jī* 立基 (foundation-laying), *Wū-yǔ* 屋宇 (buildings, with detailed elevation- and plan-illustrations of *tāng-gé*, *zhāi*, *guǎn*, *lóu*, *tái*, *gé*, *tíng*, *xiè*, *xuān*, *juǎn*, *guǎng*, *láng*, and seven roof-frame types) and *Zhuāng-zhé* 裝折 (interior partition); *juàn* 2 is on the *Lán-gān* 欄桿 (balustrades) — 100 patterns; *juàn* 3 covers *Mén-chuāng* 門窗 (doors and windows), *Qiáng-yuán* 牆垣 (walls and fences), *Pū-dì* 鋪地 (paving), *Duō-shān* 掇山 (rockwork composition), *Xuǎn-shí* 選石 (stone selection — Tài-hú-shí, Kūn-shān, Yí-xìng, Lóng-tán, Jīn-líng Qīng-lóng-shān, Líng-bì, Yīng-shí etc.), and the famous closing chapter *Jiè-jǐng* 借景 ("borrowed scenery"). The *Jiè-jǐng* chapter develops the doctrine of *jiè* (borrowing) — *yuǎn jiè* 遠借 (distant borrowing), *lín jiè* 鄰借 (neighbouring borrowing), *yǎng jiè* 仰借 (looking-up borrowing), *fǔ jiè* 俯借 (looking-down borrowing), and *yìng-shí ér jiè* 應時而借 (borrowing-according-to-season) — fundamental to all later Chinese garden theory.

### Prefaces

Three substantial paratexts frame the work, all preserved in the *krp-titles* transmission:

**Jì Chéng's self-preface (自序):** Jì recounts his life: in youth he was known as a painter, modelled himself on the Five-Dynasties landscape masters Guān Tóng 關仝 and Jīng Hào 荊浩, travelled in Yān 燕 and Chǔ 楚 lands; in middle age he returned to Wú 吳 and settled at Rùn-zhōu 潤州 (Zhèn-jiāng). There local enthusiasts collected clever rocks and piled them as fake mountains among bamboo and trees; Jì found this risible, saying: "the world hears 'where there is a real thing there is a fake of it' — why not fake the real mountain-shape, instead of *fake* clenched-fist rocks?" Asked whether he could do better, Jì constructed a *bì* 壁 (wall composition) which observers immediately recognised as a *yǎn-rán jiā shān* (a true fine mountain). He came to the notice of the *Jìn-líng Fāng-bó* (provincial intendant) Wú Yòu-yú 吳又予 of Jìn-líng, who employed him to design a 15-*mǔ* garden on the old site of Wēn-xiàng-fǔ 元溫相 (Yuán Tài-shī's old garden) east of the city. Subsequently Wāng Shì-héng 汪士衡 (a *zhōng-hàn* secretary) employed Jì at *Luán-jiāng* on the west, "matched in ambition" with Wú's project. In idle time Jì drafted the design-models, calling them *Yuán-mù* (the Garden Pastorate). Cáo Yuán-fǔ of Gū-shú, on visiting, urged Jì to retitle it *Yuán-yě* — "yours is opening a new path; let it be called *yě* (crafting) rather than *mù* (pastorate)." The self-preface is signed *Chóng-zhēn xīn-wèi zhī-qiū miǎo, Pǐ-dào rén, xián yú Hù-yě-táng zhōng tí* (the autumnal *miǎo* of *Chóng-zhēn xīn-wèi* / 1631, Pǐ-dào rén, at leisure inscribed in the *Hù-yě-táng*).

**Yuán Yú-lìng 阮于令 preface:** *Chóng-zhēn jiǎ-xū* (1634); preserved in the cut-block edition.

**Jì Chéng's postface (卷後):** "In *Chóng-zhēn jiǎ-xū* (1634), I am 53 *suì*. I have exhausted the dust of the world, weary of *yóu-yè* (sojourning), with a small *lín-xià fēng-qù* (taste of retirement-under-trees); long depending on garden-park, my interest with worldly affairs feels distant; only hearing the times' affairs in turbulence, the retiring heart is so. Ashamed of having no money to buy a mountain, I am willing to be a *Táo-yuán xī-kǒu rén* (a man of the Peach Blossom Spring stream-mouth). Sighing at this human birth's not-meeting-its-time: even Zhūgé Liàng *Wǔ-hóu* the Three-Kingdoms *shī* (teacher), Liáng-gōng (Dí Rénjié) as the *Nǚ-wáng* (Wǔ Zétiān)'s *xiàng* — the ancient *xián-háo* in their not-meeting-time; how much more this rustic and slow person. I write this *Yě* for my two sons *Cháng-shēng* and *Cháng-jí*, that they may have something like pears and chestnuts in their futures. So I have it carved and circulated, that it may be of use to the world."

### Abstract

The *Yuán-yě* is the foundation of Chinese garden theory and the single most influential premodern Chinese architectural treatise on the design of landscape gardens. Jì Chéng 計成 (b. 1582 per CBDB; death-date uncertain, with the postface establishing he was alive in 1634 aged 53; the catalog meta gives dynasty as Míng without precise dates) is one of the few documented professional garden designers in the Chinese tradition. The work was completed at the end of the Wàn-lì and the start of the Chóng-zhēn — the same generation as the late-Míng connoisseurial compendia of Zhāng Chǒu [[KR3h0055]] and Wāng Kěyù [[KR3h0060]], and the architectural treatises of the Sòng-Yìng-zào-fǎ-shì tradition. The work is divided into theoretical exposition (the *Xìng-zào lùn* of *juàn* 1, the *Jiè-jǐng* of *juàn* 3 close) and practical instruction with extensive engraved illustrations (building-frames, balustrade patterns, door-and-window patterns, wall patterns, rock-stacking diagrams). The work fell into relative neglect in the early Qīng (the Sìkù editors did not include it, perhaps because of Jì's association with the Wú Yòu-yú garden-construction patronage network), but was rediscovered by Japanese architects (the *Yuán-yě* was preserved in Japan, where it influenced Edo-period garden theory), and reintroduced to Chinese scholarship in the 1930s by Yuán Yěshēng 元冶生 / Liú Dūnzhēn 劉敦楨 and others. The work's three foundational concepts — *yīn* 因 (according-with: working with the site), *jiè* 借 (borrowing: integrating outside views), and *tǐ-yí* 體宜 (body-and-fittingness: appropriate scale) — became the canonical vocabulary of all later Chinese garden criticism.

## Other points of interest

The *Yuán-yě* is the only major Chinese garden-design treatise to survive in pre-modern China complete with technical illustrations, and the only pre-modern Chinese architectural treatise to give a systematic poetics of practice. The discovery of the *Yuán-yě* by Japanese architectural historians in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and its retro-introduction to Chinese architectural scholarship in the 1930s, is itself a major episode in modern Sino-Japanese cultural history. The work has been the principal source for the modern restoration and reconstruction of Jiāngnán private gardens (Sūzhōu, Wú-xī, Yáng-zhōu) from the 1950s onwards.
