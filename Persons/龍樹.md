Nāgārjuna (Lóngshù 龍樹, "Dragon-Tree"; alternate Chinese 龍勝, *Lóngshèng*, "Dragon-Victorious") is the most consequential figure of the Mahāyāna Buddhist philosophical tradition. Conventionally dated to the second century CE (c. 150 – 250) — though more recent scholarship (Jan Yün-hua, Joseph Walser, Tom Tillemans) has suggested both earlier and later possibilities, with the secure historical anchor being the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* and a small core of associated texts — Nāgārjuna is credited as the founder of the *Madhyamaka* (Middle Way) school of Buddhist philosophy.

The Chinese tradition (per the *Lóng-shù pútísà zhuàn* 龍樹菩薩傳, T2047, an early-fifth-century Chinese hagiography by Kumārajīva or attributed to him) places Nāgārjuna in South India (Vidarbha or further south); a *brāhmaṇa* by birth, he was famously associated with the *nāga* palace from which (per the legend) he retrieved the *[[KR6e0001|Avataṃsaka-sūtra]]* (the legend reproduced by [[吉藏]] Jízàng in his *[[KR6e0002|Huāyán yóu yì]]*) and the *Prajñāpāramitā* literature.

His most consequential extant works are: the *Mūlamadhyamaka-kārikā* 中論 (T1564, in Kumārajīva's translation by [[鳩摩羅什]] Kumārajīva); the *Vigrahavyāvartanī* (preserved only in Tibetan and Sanskrit, with no Chinese translation); the *Yuktiṣaṣṭikā* (T1575, in Tang translation); the *Śūnyatāsaptati*; the *Dvādaśadvāra-śāstra* 十二門論 (T1568, Kumārajīva); the *Mahāprajñāpāramitā-śāstra* / *Dà zhì dù lùn* 大智度論 (T1509, Kumārajīva, the great commentary on the *Pañcaviṃśati-prajñāpāramitā*); the *[[KR6e0059|Daśabhūmika-vibhāṣā / Shí zhù pípóshā lùn 十住毘婆沙論]]* (T1521, Kumārajīva, the great commentary on the *Daśabhūmika*-related material); and a very large corpus of less-well-attested or pseudepigraphic works.

Nāgārjuna's doctrinal contribution — the doctrine of the *two truths* (*conventional* and *ultimate*), the doctrine of *emptiness* (*śūnyatā*) of all dharmas, and the rigorous application of dialectical reasoning to the metaphysical concepts of Indian Buddhist philosophy — is the foundation of all subsequent East Asian Mahāyāna scholasticism. In China his influence is direct (through Kumārajīva's translations of his works, which provided the basis for the Sān-lùn 三論 / Three-Treatise school) and indirect (through the Mahāyāna doctrinal apparatus that he shaped). In the Huáyán tradition he is credited with retrieving the *Avataṃsaka* itself from the *nāga* palace; in Tantric Buddhism he is credited with founding several Tantric lineages. In Tibet he is the lineage-founder of the *dBu ma* (Madhyamaka) tradition.
