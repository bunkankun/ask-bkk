# Yáng Mǎnuò 陽瑪諾 / Manuel Dias the Younger, S.J.

Portuguese Jesuit. Born Castelo Branco (Beira province, Portugal), 1574; died Hángzhōu, 4 March 1659. The Chinese name *Yáng Mǎnuò* renders the personal name "Emanuel" (*Mǎnuò* = Emmanuel) with the *Yáng* surname (a free choice, occasionally rendered *Diāz* or other transcriptions of "Dias"). Distinguished from his older Jesuit colleague **Manuel Dias the Elder** (1559–1639), the *Lǐ Mǎnuò* 李瑪諾 of late-Wànlì-period sources who served as Vice-Provincial of the China mission — the two are not to be confused.

Joined the Society of Jesus 1592 in Coimbra; sent to Asia 1601, arriving Macao the same year and Beijing 1611 — the year of Sabatino de Ursis's *Jiǎnpíng yí shuō* ([[KR3f0011]]) and Xú Guāngqǐ's foundational preface, the moment when the Jesuit-Chinese mathematical-astronomical synthesis was being consolidated. Successive China stations: Beijing, Nánjīng (until the 1616 anti-Christian persecution), then various south-China residences after his return from temporary deportation; Hángzhōu in his last decades.

Trained in mathematics and theology at the Roman College; brought to the China mission strong competence in scholastic philosophy and astronomy. His chief contributions to the Chinese-language Jesuit corpus:

(1) **The *Tiānwèn lüè* 天問略 ([[KR3f0012]])** of Wànlì *yǐmǎo* (1615): a question-and-answer cosmographic-and-astronomical treatise expounding the twelve-celestial-spheres model (extending the nine-spheres of Ricci's [[KR3f0009]] *Qiánkūn tǐyì* with three additional spheres above, the twelfth being the immobile Empyrean — *immobile* heaven of the saints), the seven planetary positions, the seasonal-mechanism, the eclipse theory, and tabular data on day-length and twilight at different latitudes for fifteen Míng provinces. The work was the most popular and accessible of the Wànlì-period Jesuit cosmographic books, and one of the few to reach a wider audience beyond the inner Jesuit-affiliated literati circle.

(2) **The *Shèngjīng zhíjiě* 聖經直解** (Hángzhōu, 1636/1640): the first Chinese-language Catholic commentary on the four Gospels — selections-with-commentary structured around the Sunday and feast-day Mass readings — which would remain the standard Chinese-Catholic Gospel commentary into the 19th century.

(3) **The *Qīngshì jīnshū* 輕世金書** (1640): a Chinese rendering of Thomas à Kempis's *De imitatione Christi* (Books I–III), one of the most important Catholic ascetical-spiritual texts ever produced in Chinese.

(4) **Catechetical-and-doctrinal works**: *Tiānzhǔ shèngjiào shíjiè zhíquán* 天主聖教十誡直詮; *Dàiyí piān* 代疑篇; *Tàixī jǐngcǎo* 太西景教碑頌正詮 (commentary on the recently rediscovered Xī'ān Nestorian stele of 781).

After the 1616 persecution Yáng Mǎnuò was one of the principal Jesuits who maintained the China mission's academic-and-spiritual life through the difficult Tiānqǐ and early-Chóngzhēn years; he survived into the early Qīng period and saw the consolidation of the post-Schall court astronomy. His *Tiānwèn lüè* 提要 in the *Sìkù* records one of the more candid Qián-lóng-period editorial interventions: the editors openly state that they removed Yáng's original preface (which insisted that the unmoving twelfth heaven was where God and the saints reside) on the grounds that "*its purport is extremely deceitful*" but preserved the underlying astronomical exposition as "*reliable and based on evidence*" — a vivid illustration of the late-Qīng editorial separation of Jesuit *science* (preserved) from Jesuit *theology* (excised).
