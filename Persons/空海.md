The **founder of the Japanese Shingon school** 真言 — one of the two founding figures (with **Saichō** 最澄) of the **Heian-era Buddhist establishment**. Japanese name **Kūkai** 空海; posthumous title **Kōbō Daishi** 弘法大師 ("Great Master of the Spreading of the Dharma"). Born **774 CE** in **Sanuki Province** (modern Shikoku); died **835 CE** at his **Mount Kōya** 高野山 monastery.

He undertook the foundational Tang study mission of **804–806** — together with **Saichō** in the same Japanese embassy fleet — bringing back to Japan the complete **Esoteric** (*mantra-yāna* / *vajra-yāna*) doctrinal-ritual apparatus that became the foundation of Japanese **Shingon** Buddhism.

His Tang study took place at **Chángān** under **Huìguǒ** 惠果 (746–805), the eighth Esoteric patriarch and direct disciple of **Bùkōng** 不空 (705–774, Amoghavajra). From Huìguǒ in 805 he received the **complete dual-mandala transmission** (the **Garbhadhātu** 胎藏界 / Womb-realm and **Vajradhātu** 金剛界 / Diamond-realm Esoteric initiations) — making him the **direct lineage-heir** of the entire Tang Esoteric tradition. Huìguǒ died later that same year (805); Kūkai returned to Japan in 806.

He documented his importation in the catalog:

- **Yù qǐnglái mùlù** 御請來目錄 ([[KR6s0107]], T2161) — the principal documentation of his 805–806 importation, comprising 216 sutras / treatises (462 juan total) plus elaborate Esoteric ritual implements, mandala paintings, and other ceremonial materials.

On returning to Japan, he received the imperial-court favor of the early-Heian emperors (Saga 嵯峨 and Junna 淳和) and established the **Shingon school** institutional framework. His principal monastic establishments were:

- **Mount Kōya** 高野山 — the **Kōya-san** monastic complex in Wakayama, established in **816** as the principal Shingon scholarly-monastic center.
- **Tō-ji** 東寺 in Kyoto — the imperial Esoteric temple, granted to Kūkai by Emperor Saga in **823** as the official imperial Shingon ceremony center.

His doctrinal output is foundational for Japanese Shingon:

- **Dèngyō wènyì** 顯密二教論 (*Differences between the Apparent and Esoteric Teachings*).
- **Shízhùxīnlùn** 十住心論 (*Treatise on the Ten Levels of Mind*).
- **Shēngzì shíxiàng yì** 聲字實相義 (*The Meanings of Sound, Letter, and Reality*).
- **Yùzhī shíjiàn yánrén** 即身成佛義 (*Becoming a Buddha in This Very Body*).
- And many other doctrinal-philosophical works.

He is also among the most consequential figures of pre-modern Japanese cultural history, credited with the invention of the **kana** syllabary (the *Iroha* 47-character alphabet poem traditionally attributed to him), the introduction of substantial Chinese Tang cultural elements (calligraphy, lexicography, ritual technology) to Japan, and the establishment of the **Shikoku Pilgrimage** (the 88-temple pilgrimage circuit on his birthplace island).

He was posthumously titled **Kōbō Daishi** 弘法大師 in **921** — over a century after his death — and is venerated as one of the principal Buddhist sage-figures in Japanese religious tradition. The hagiographical tradition holds that he is **not dead** but in eternal *samādhi* in his Mount Kōya tomb, awaiting the coming of **Maitreya**.
