Yǐnyuán Lóngqí 隱元隆琦 (Wànlì 20.11.4 = 7 December 1592 – Japanese Kan-bun 癸丑 4.3 = Kāngxī 12.4.4 = 19 May 1673), *zì* **Yǐnyuán** 隱元, self-styled **Shānyě** 山野, lay surname **Lín** 林, lay name **Lín Zǐfáng** 林子房 / **Lín Zēngbǐng** 林曾昺. The founder of the Japanese **Ōbaku** 黃檗 school (the third of the three major Zen sects of early-modern Japan, alongside Rinzai and Sōtō), the last major émigré Chán patriarch to Japan, and posthumously the recipient of three imperial Japanese honorific titles: **大光普照國師**, **覺性圓明國師**, and (on the 350th anniversary of his death, conferred by the Reiwa emperor in 2022 as the first "plural-state-teacher" designation in modern imperial history) **嚴統大師**.

Native of **Fúqīng** 福清 (Fújiàn), Lóngqí was the principal dharma-heir of **Fèiyǐn Tōngróng** [[通容]] 費隱通容 (1593–1661), through whom he received the LínjìYángqí line of **Mìyún Yuánwù** [[圓悟]] 密雲圓悟 (1566/7–1642). Tonsured under Jiànyuán (*Jiànyuán gōng* 鑑源公) at a local Huáng-bò-area monastery; first travelled to **Jīnsù** 金粟 to see Mìyún, then settled at **Huángbò Wànfúchánsì** 黃檗山萬福禪寺 under Fèiyǐn, who transmitted the dharma to him in Chóngzhēn *dīngchǒu* (1637) winter and handed over the Huángbò abbacy. Lóngqí next held **Chóngdé Fúyán** 崇德福嚴 and **Chánglè Lóngquán** 長樂龍泉 before the invitation from the Japanese **Kōfuku-ji** 興福寺 community of Nagasaki reached him in Shùnzhì 11 *jiǎwǔ* (1654) autumn — whereupon he and a group of Chinese disciples crossed to **Nagasaki**, then to **Fumon-ji** 普門寺 near Osaka, and finally in Kan-bun 1 (1661) founded a new **Huángbòshān Wànfúsì** 黃檗山萬福寺 at **Uji** 宇治 south of Kyōto — the Japanese mother-temple of the Ōbaku school — on land granted by the *shōgun* Tokugawa Ietsuna after imperial-court backing from Emperor Go-Sai. He died in residence there 19 May 1673, aged 81 *sùi*.

Important works: a multi-juan **《隱元禪師語錄》** (partly in Kanripo as sub-items of the Ōbaku corpus), a **《弘戒法儀》** *Hóngjiè fǎyí* regulating Japanese Ōbaku ordinations in the Chinese Línjì pattern, a **《普照國師年譜》** compiled by his heirs, and numerous poem-collections. Key surviving dharma-heirs listed in DILA: **Huìmén Pèi** 慧門沛 (Huángbò in China), **Xūbái Yuàn** 虗白願 (Huángbò), **Wúdé Níng** 無得寧 (Níngdé Yùxiàng Lónghuá), **Liángyě Lè** 良冶樂 (Chāoshān in Mǐnnán), and **Zhōngzhù Dǐ** 中柱砥 (Shèngquán in Fúzhōu) — to which must be added the Japanese-side heirs, especially **Mùān Xìngtāo** 木庵性瑫 (Mokuan Shōtō, 1611–1684) who succeeded him as second Japanese Manpuku-ji abbot and continued the Chinese-language transmission line.

Notable Kanripo work for which Lóngqí is the nominal editor: [[KR6q0403]] 《費隱禪師語錄》 14 juan (J26 B178) — the posthumous Jiāxīng recension of his master's sayings, in which his "*děngbiān* 等編" credit at the top of juan 1 is a transmission-marker rather than a post-1661 editorial hand (Lóngqí was by then resident in Japan and could not have supervised the Jiāxīng cutting directly). The text is a primary documentary object for the Fèiyǐn → Lóngqí → Ōbaku dharma-link and for the pre-1654 Chinese phase of Ōbaku's emergence.

Principal secondary literature: **Baroni, Helen J.**, *Obaku Zen: The Emergence of the Third Sect of Zen in Tokugawa Japan* (Honolulu: University of Hawai'i Press, 2000); **Jiang Wu**, *Leaving for the Rising Sun: Chinese Zen Master Yinyuan and the Authenticity Crisis in Early Modern East Asia* (Oxford University Press, 2015) — the definitive English-language monograph specifically on Yǐnyuán; **Rǎo Zōngyí** 饒宗頤 (ed.), 《隱元全集》 20 vols (Kyōto: Manpuku-ji, 1979); and **Chén Zhìpíng** 陳智平, 《隱元隆琦禪學思想研究》 (Beijing: Zōngjiào wénhuà chūbǎnshè, 2018).
