Yǐn-yuán Lóng-qí 隱元隆琦 (Wàn-lì 20.11.4 = 7 December 1592 – Japanese Kan-bun 癸丑 4.3 = Kāng-xī 12.4.4 = 19 May 1673), *zì* **Yǐn-yuán** 隱元, self-styled **Shān-yě** 山野, lay surname **Lín** 林, lay name **Lín Zǐ-fáng** 林子房 / **Lín Zēng-bǐng** 林曾昺. The founder of the Japanese **Ōbaku** 黃檗 school (the third of the three major Zen sects of early-modern Japan, alongside Rinzai and Sōtō), the last major émigré Chán patriarch to Japan, and posthumously the recipient of three imperial Japanese honorific titles: **大光普照國師**, **覺性圓明國師**, and (on the 350th anniversary of his death, conferred by the Reiwa emperor in 2022 as the first "plural-state-teacher" designation in modern imperial history) **嚴統大師**.

Native of **Fú-qīng** 福清 (Fú-jiàn), Lóng-qí was the principal dharma-heir of **Fèi-yǐn Tōng-róng** [[通容]] 費隱通容 (1593–1661), through whom he received the Línjì-Yángqí line of **Mìyún Yuánwù** [[圓悟]] 密雲圓悟 (1566/7–1642). Tonsured under Jiàn-yuán (*Jiàn-yuán gōng* 鑑源公) at a local Huáng-bò-area monastery; first travelled to **Jīn-sù** 金粟 to see Mìyún, then settled at **Huáng-bò Wàn-fú-chán-sì** 黃檗山萬福禪寺 under Fèi-yǐn, who transmitted the dharma to him in Chóng-zhēn *dīng-chǒu* (1637) winter and handed over the Huáng-bò abbacy. Lóng-qí next held **Chóng-dé Fú-yán** 崇德福嚴 and **Cháng-lè Lóng-quán** 長樂龍泉 before the invitation from the Japanese **Kōfuku-ji** 興福寺 community of Nagasaki reached him in Shùn-zhì 11 *jiǎ-wǔ* (1654) autumn — whereupon he and a group of Chinese disciples crossed to **Nagasaki**, then to **Fumon-ji** 普門寺 near Osaka, and finally in Kan-bun 1 (1661) founded a new **Huáng-bò-shān Wàn-fú-sì** 黃檗山萬福寺 at **Uji** 宇治 south of Kyōto — the Japanese mother-temple of the Ōbaku school — on land granted by the *shōgun* Tokugawa Ietsuna after imperial-court backing from Emperor Go-Sai. He died in residence there 19 May 1673, aged 81 *sùi*.

Important works: a multi-juan **《隱元禪師語錄》** (partly in Kanripo as sub-items of the Ōbaku corpus), a **《弘戒法儀》** *Hóng-jiè fǎ-yí* regulating Japanese Ōbaku ordinations in the Chinese Línjì pattern, a **《普照國師年譜》** compiled by his heirs, and numerous poem-collections. Key surviving dharma-heirs listed in DILA: **Huì-mén Pèi** 慧門沛 (Huáng-bò in China), **Xū-bái Yuàn** 虗白願 (Huáng-bò), **Wú-dé Níng** 無得寧 (Níng-dé Yù-xiàng Lóng-huá), **Liáng-yě Lè** 良冶樂 (Chāo-shān in Mǐn-nán), and **Zhōng-zhù Dǐ** 中柱砥 (Shèng-quán in Fú-zhōu) — to which must be added the Japanese-side heirs, especially **Mù-ān Xìng-tāo** 木庵性瑫 (Mokuan Shōtō, 1611–1684) who succeeded him as second Japanese Manpuku-ji abbot and continued the Chinese-language transmission line.

Notable Kanripo work for which Lóng-qí is the nominal editor: [[KR6q0403]] 《費隱禪師語錄》 14 juan (J26 B178) — the posthumous Jiā-xīng recension of his master's sayings, in which his "*děng-biān* 等編" credit at the top of juan 1 is a transmission-marker rather than a post-1661 editorial hand (Lóng-qí was by then resident in Japan and could not have supervised the Jiā-xīng cutting directly). The text is a primary documentary object for the Fèi-yǐn → Lóng-qí → Ōbaku dharma-link and for the pre-1654 Chinese phase of Ōbaku's emergence.

Principal secondary literature: **Baroni, Helen J.**, *Obaku Zen: The Emergence of the Third Sect of Zen in Tokugawa Japan* (Honolulu: University of Hawai'i Press, 2000); **Jiang Wu**, *Leaving for the Rising Sun: Chinese Zen Master Yinyuan and the Authenticity Crisis in Early Modern East Asia* (Oxford University Press, 2015) — the definitive English-language monograph specifically on Yǐn-yuán; **Rǎo Zōng-yí** 饒宗頤 (ed.), 《隱元全集》 20 vols (Kyōto: Manpuku-ji, 1979); and **Chén Zhì-píng** 陳智平, 《隱元隆琦禪學思想研究》 (Beijing: Zōng-jiào wén-huà chū-bǎn-shè, 2018).
