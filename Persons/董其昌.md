Dǒng Qí-chāng 董其昌 (1555–1636), *zì* Yuán-zǎi 元宰 (also written 玄宰), *hào* Sī-bái 思白, Xiāng-guāng jū-shì 香光居士; posthumous title Wén-mǐn 文敏. Native of Huá-tíng 華亭 (Sōng-jiāng prefecture, modern Shànghǎi). *Jìnshì* of *Wànlì* 17 / *jǐ-chǒu* (1589); appointed *shù-jí-shì* (Hanlin bachelor). Rose through the *Zhān-shì fǔ* (Office of Imperial Tutors) to *Lǐ-bù shàng-shū* (Minister of Rites), with the additional honorific *Tài-zǐ tài-bǎo*. Biography in *Míng shǐ · Wén-yuàn zhuàn*.

Dǒng is by far the most influential Chinese art theorist of the 17th century — the architect of the *nán běi èr zōng* (Southern–Northern Two Schools) periodization of literati painting and the dominant calligrapher of the late Míng. His chief theoretical writings are gathered in the *Huà-chán shì suí-bǐ* 畫禪室隨筆 ([[KR3j0158]]); his collected literary writings are the *Róng-tái jí* 容臺集 (which the Sìkù editors recorded in their *cún mù*). He was also a major art collector and connoisseur. His career was politically and socially controversial — the 1616 *mín chāo Dǒng huàn* (Citizens' Sacking of Dǒng) riots against his household at Huá-tíng are documented in the *Míng-Qīng historical record* and discussed at length in modern scholarship (Wai-kam Ho, *The Century of Tung Ch'i-ch'ang*).
