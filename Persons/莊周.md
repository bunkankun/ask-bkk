The historical figure behind the philosophical masterwork **[[KR5c0051|*Nánhuá zhēn jīng*]]** 南華真經 (the *Zhuāngzǐ* 莊子). Known to ancient biography almost entirely through the notice at *Shǐjì* 史記 63.2143–45 and a handful of incidental references in the *Hán Fēi zǐ* 韓非子 and the *Lǚshì chūnqiū* 呂氏春秋.

**Native place and office.** The *Shǐjì* records that Zhuāng Zhōu was a man of **Měng** 蒙, a township in the ancient state of **Sòng** 宋 near modern Shāngqiū 商丘 in eastern Hénán, at the intersection of present-day Hénán, Shāndōng, Jiāngsū, and Ānhuī. He held the minor office of **functionary at the Lacquer Garden** (*Qī yuán lì* 漆園吏) — the seat of a state-managed lacquer plantation — before retiring to the life of an impecunious philosopher. After the fall of Sòng in 286 BCE, the Měng region fell to Chǔ 楚, and Zhuāng Zhōu is therefore variably described in later sources as a man of Sòng or a man of Chǔ.

**Dates.** The *Shǐjì* places Zhuāng Zhōu's floruit in the reigns of **King Huì of Wèi** 魏惠王 (r. c. 369–325 BCE) and **King Wēi of Chǔ** 楚威王 (r. c. 339–328 BCE), and records that King Wēi invited him to court with offers of high office — which Zhuāng Zhōu famously refused, comparing the prospect to that of a sacrificial ox. These anchor points place him in the second half of the fourth century BCE, making him roughly contemporary with **Mèngzǐ** 孟子 (c. 372–289 BCE). The standard modern tentative dates — given by Liú Xiàogǎn 劉笑敢 and widely accepted — are **c. 369–286 BCE** (Liú, *Classifying the Zhuangzi Chapters* 41). Earlier lifedate proposals include c. 369–298 BCE (Qián Mù 錢穆) and the simpler "second half of the fourth century" (Schipper 2004, 1:60).

**The work under his name.** Tradition ascribes to him the entire *Zhuāngzǐ* in its *Hàn shū, Yìwén zhì* 漢書藝文志 form of **52 *piān*** (100,000+ characters). The received 33-chapter redaction of **[[郭象]]** (Guō Xiàng, d. 312) reduces this to ≈70,000 characters; virtually none of the excised 19 *piān* survives independently. Since Liú Xiàogǎn's foundational stratigraphy and A. C. Graham's subsequent analysis, scholarly consensus holds that only the seven *Nèi piān* 內篇 (1. *Xiāoyáo yóu* 逍遥遊, 2. *Qí wù lùn* 齊物論, 3. *Yǎng shēng zhǔ* 養生主, 4. *Rén jiān shì* 人閒世, 5. *Dé chōng fú* 德充符, 6. *Dà zōng shī* 大宗師, 7. *Yìng dì wáng* 應帝王) can be confidently attributed to Zhuāng Zhōu himself; the *Wài piān* 外篇 and *Zá piān* 雜篇 are the work of his immediate and extended philosophical descendants, with late additions continuing into the early Hàn.

**Canonisation.** Táng Xuánzōng 唐玄宗 in 742 CE enfeoffed Zhuāng Zhōu posthumously as ***Nánhuá zhēn rén*** 南華真人 ("Perfected of the Southern Florescence"), and decreed that the book be called *Nánhuá zhēn jīng*. The title refers at once to the cosmological flight southward of the *Péng* 鵬 bird in *Xiāoyáo yóu* and to the later Daoist hagiographic identification of Zhuāng Zhōu's retirement place as Nánhuá Shān 南華山 in Cáozhōu 曹州 (modern Hézé, Shāndōng). He is one of the four disciples of Lǎozǐ 老子 canonised simultaneously under the Táng — the others being Lièzǐ ([[列禦寇]]), Wénzǐ, and Kàngcāng zǐ ([[庚桑楚]]).

**Philosophical legacy.** The *Zhuāngzǐ* has had a definitive influence on the entire subsequent Chinese tradition — not only on philosophical Daoism (by way of Wèi-Jìn *xuánxué* 玄學, especially Guō Xiàng and Xiàng Xiù 向秀), but equally on Chán 禪 Buddhism (the butterfly-dream, the cook's ox, the useless tree, and the drilling of Húndùn 渾沌 are all *gōng àn* 公案 staples), on the literati aesthetic of *xiāoyáo* 逍遥 freedom, and on the broader *sān jiào* 三教 synthesis of the late imperial period. On the strength of the *Nèi piān* alone, Zhuāng Zhōu is generally regarded — with Lǎozǐ and Kǒngzǐ — as one of the three most influential philosophical minds of pre-Imperial China.
