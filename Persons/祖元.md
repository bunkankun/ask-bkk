**Wúxué Zǔyuán** 無學祖元 / **子元祖元** (Jp. Mugaku Sogen, 1226–1286) — Late-Sòng Chinese **Línjì-school** Chán master who emigrated to Japan in 1279 and became the **founder of Engaku-ji** 圓覺寺 in Kamakura — one of the *Five-Mountain* (五山) temples of the Kamakura system. Style-name **Ziyuan** 子元, sobriquet **Wuxue** 無學 ("Beyond Learning"). Posthumous imperial title **Bukkō Zenji** 佛光禪師 ("Buddha-Light Zen Master"); later elevated by **Emperor Jōji** 貞治帝 (Go-Murakami) to **Bukkō Enman Jōshō Kokushi** 佛光圓滿常照國師 ("Buddha-Light Universally-Complete Ever-Illuminating National Master").

Born **Bǎoqìng 2 (1226)** in **Mìng-zhōu** 明州 (modern Níngbō, Zhèjiāng) — per *Bukkō Zenji tōmei* 佛光禪師塔銘 (his stūpa-inscription preserved in fasc. 9 of his *yulu*). Received Línjì-school transmission from **Wúzhǔn Shīfàn** 無準師範 (1178–1249) at Jìngshān-sì 徑山寺 — like [[圓爾]] Enni Ben'en before him. Travelled and taught at Tiānzhēn-sì 天眞寺, Tāizhōu Zhēn-rú-sì 台州真如寺, and other major Sòng monasteries.

In **Kōan 2 (1279)**, with the Mongol invasions threatening south China, he travelled to Japan at the invitation of the regent **Hōjō Tokimune** 北條時宗. Welcomed at **Kenchō-ji** 建長寺 in Kamakura; in **Kōan 5 (1282)** Tokimune founded **Engaku-ji** 圓覺寺 to commemorate the war-dead of the Mongol invasions, with Wúxué as founding abbot. Died at Engaku-ji on the **3rd day of the 9th month, Kōan 9 (1286)**, age 61 (per his stūpa-inscription).

His Recorded Sayings — *Bukkō Kokushi goroku* 佛光國師語錄 ([[KR6t0255]], T80n2549) — ten fascicles, compiled by **Yīzhēn** 一真 and others; redacted with an extensive preface that situates him alongside Confucius, Mencius, and the Buddha at Vulture-Peak as a "universal" sage. The work is one of the most elaborate Japanese-period *yulu* of the Five-Mountain era. His Japanese disciple **Kōhō Kennichi** 高峰顯日 was the master of [[#夢窓疎石|Musō Soseki]].

Source: *Bukkō Zenji tōmei*; *Bukkō Kokushi goroku* preface; standard Japanese-Zen biographical sources; **Dumoulin**, *Zen Buddhism, vol. 2: Japan*, pp. 30, 36; **Collcutt, Martin**, *Five Mountains* (1981).
