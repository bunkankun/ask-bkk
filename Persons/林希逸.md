Southern Sòng 南宋 scholar-official, Neo-Confucian-trained yet substantially Daoist-and-Buddhist in scholarly interest — one of the most influential Daoist commentators of the period, whose ***kǒu yì*** 口義 ("oral explanations") commentaries on the three classical Daoist texts (*Lǎozǐ*, *Zhuāngzǐ*, *Lièzǐ*) shaped Southern-Sòng, Yuán, Míng, Chosǒn Korean, and Edo Japanese reception of the Daoist classical corpus.

**Lifedates.** Variously given as **c. 1193–1271** (primary modern scholarly dating; CBDB 11087 has 0–0 placeholder) or **c. 1210–c. 1273**. *Zì* **Sù wēng** 肅翁; *hào* **Juàn zhāi** 鬳齋 ("Three-Legged-Cauldron Studio") or **Zhú xī** 竹溪 ("Bamboo Creek"). Native of **Fú qīng** 福清 (modern Fújiàn 福建).

**Career.** *Jìnshì* of the **Duān píng** 端平 era (1234), under Lǐ zōng 理宗. Rose steadily through the scholar-official ranks. Held successively:

- **Mì shū xǐng** 祕書省 positions (Imperial Library).
- **Zhōng shū shè rén** 中書舍人 (Drafter of the Central Secretariat).
- **Hàn lín xué shì** 翰林學士 (Hànlín Academician) in the late 1260s.
- Retired in his 60s.

**Scholarly identity.** Lín Xīyì was **first and foremost a Confucian scholar-official** (Robinet's characterisation, Schipper & Verellen 2004, 2:2372), trained in the mainstream Zhū Xī 朱熹-inflected Neo-Confucian orthodoxy of his era. However, his scholarly engagement extended substantially into Daoist and Buddhist textual traditions — an ecumenism characteristic of the late-Southern-Sòng literati culture.

**Principal works.** His three *kǒu yì* commentaries on the classical Daoist texts form a **coordinated scholarly project**:

1. **[[KR5c0088|*Dàodé zhēn jīng kǒu yì*]]** 道德真經口義 (DZ 701, 1261) — 4 *juàn*. The *Lǎozǐ* commentary.
2. ***Nán huá zhēn jīng kǒu yì*** 南華真經口義 (DZ 745) — the *Zhuāngzǐ* commentary. Translated into Edo-era Japanese and highly influential on Ogyū Sorai 荻生徂徠 and other 17th-18th-century Japanese scholars.
3. ***Chōng xū zhí dé zhēn jīng kǒu yì*** 沖虛至德真經口義 — the *Lièzǐ* commentary.

Also attested:

4. ***Juàn zhāi Xù jí*** 鬳齋續集 — poetry and prose collection.
5. ***Zhú xī Juàn zhāi shí yī gǎo*** 竹溪鬳齋十一藁 — a 22-*juàn* collection of poetry, memorials, and essays. Preserved in the Wén yuān gé *Sìkù quánshū*.
6. **Commentary on the *Chǔ cí*** 楚辭 — partially preserved.

**Scholarly style.** The *kǒu yì* ("oral explanations") genre is Lín Xīyì's distinctive contribution to late-imperial commentarial literature. His approach:

- **Vernacular-register prose** — pedagogically accessible rather than scholastically precise.
- **Explicit acknowledgment of the Daoist classics' distinct language-system** — "the *Lǎozǐ* and the *Zhuāngzǐ* can only be read by applying criteria and values different from those appropriate to Confucian texts" (see [[KR5c0088]]).
- **Integration of Confucian, Buddhist, and Daoist terminology** into a unified interpretive framework.
- **Systematic cross-reference between the three Daoist classics**, treating them as mutually-interpretive.

**Influence.** The *kǒu yì* commentaries enjoyed an extraordinary influence across the East Asian sphere:

- **China**: Widely reprinted in the Yuán and Míng; standard reading for Southern-Sòng and later literati.
- **Korea**: Standard reading in **Chosǒn** (1392–1897) literati circles; multiple reprints.
- **Japan**: Dominant in **Edo-era** (1603–1868) Daoist scholarship; shaped the readings of Ogyū Sorai, Itō Jinsai 伊藤仁齋, and other major Japanese Confucian-Daoist figures.
