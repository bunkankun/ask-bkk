# Dù-yáng zá-biān 杜陽雜編
*Miscellaneous Compilation from Du-yang*
by [[蘇鶚]] (撰)

## About the work

A three-*juàn* late-Táng anecdotal-anomaly compilation by Sū È 蘇鶚 (*zì* Dé-xiáng 德祥, *fl.* late 9th c.; *jìn-shì* of the *Guāng-qǐ* 光啟 reign-period, 885–888), spanning the reigns of *ten emperors* — Dài-zōng 代宗 (763) through Yì-zōng 懿宗 (873–4) — and titled after his native village Dù-yáng 杜陽 in Wǔ-gōng 武功 (modern Shǎn-xī). Each entry takes a three-character heading and centres on (i) an extraordinary tribute-object (*gòng-wù* 貢物) supposedly presented to the Táng court by some named outer-realm kingdom, (ii) a court anecdote about the emperor's reception of it, and (iii) the moral or aesthetic point Sū draws. The book is the principal repository for the fabulous tribute-culture of the late Táng — fragrant silks, fire-resistant jades, glowing pearls, automata, dwarf horses, and so on — and was already by the Sòng a major mine for *lèi-shū* compilers and *cí*-poets. The *Sìkù* compilers candidly note that many of the supposed tributary states are unattested in the *Táng shū* and that the geographical and chronological claims are frequently impossible (e.g., the Fū-yú 夫餘 kingdom, long absorbed into Bó-hǎi by the 9th c., is described as paying tribute in 841), but they retain it for its rich vocabulary and stylistic value — "the *xiǎo-shuō* writer who wins by literary colour" (*xiǎo-shuō-jiā zhī yǐ wén-cǎi shèng zhě* 小說家之以文采勝者).

### Tiyao

Your servants report: *Dù-yáng zá-biān* in 3 *juàn*. The Táng Sū È 蘇鶚 *zhuàn*. È is the author of the *Yǎn-yì* 演義, already separately catalogued. This compilation covers from Dài-zōng *Guǎng-dé* 1 (763) at the top down to Yì-zōng *Xián-tōng* 14 (873) — the affairs of ten reigns. All entries take three-character headings. Within, the descriptions of strange crafts and precious objects are of the kind that strays beyond the standard. In general, it descends from Wáng Jiā's 王嘉 *Shí-yí jì* 拾遺記 and Guō Zǐ-héng's 郭子橫 *Dòng-míng jì* 洞冥記 — though it always cites the source-person to substantiate, this is yet only the way colloquial talk turns into bright pigment. The names it gives — *Rì-lín* 日林, *Dà-lín* 大林, *Wén-dān* 文單, *Wū-míng* 烏明, *Jū-mí* 拘弭, *Dà-zhěn* 大軫, *Nán-chāng* 南昌, *Zhè-dōng* 淛東, *Tiáo-zhī* 條支, *Guǐ-gǔ* 鬼谷, *Hē-líng* 訶陵, *Dōu-lí* 兜離 (claiming each as the donor of a certain object in a certain year) — the *Táng shū* *Wài-guó zhuàn* gives none of these names, nor do the various imperial annals carry the supposed events. To take one example: the Fū-yú 夫餘 kingdom was long since absorbed by Bó-hǎi 渤海's Dà 大 clan, yet it claims that in Wǔ-zōng *Huì-chāng* 1 (841) Fū-yú came to pay tribute. As for Jì-bīn 罽賓 — which borders on the Cōng-lǐng [Pamirs], as both the *Hàn shū* and the *Táng shū* state plainly — it is here said to lie in the Western Sea. These are clearly egregious. Yet its elaborate, ornate display is constantly mined for material by poets and *fù*-writers: it is the *xiǎo-shuō* writer who wins by literary colour. The reader sucks in its sweet petals and so forgets its exaggerations; even today the citations continue. The name *Dù-yáng zá-biān*: Cháo Gōng-wǔ's 晁公武 *Dú-shū zhì* says that È lived at Dù-yáng of Wǔ-gōng — he therefore named his book after the place.

Respectfully checked, *Qián-lóng* 46 (1781), 9th month. Chief Compilers: Jì Yún 紀昀, Lù Xī-xióng 陸錫熊, Sūn Shì-yì 孫士毅. Chief Collator: Lù Fèi-chí 陸費墀.

### Abstract

The composition window adopted here is 874–886. The lower bound is fixed by the latest event the book covers — Yì-zōng *Xián-tōng* 14 (873), the year of Yì-zōng's death — which must have been written at the earliest in 874; the upper bound is fixed by Sū È's other surviving work, the *Sū-shì yǎn-yì* 蘇氏演義 [[KR3j0028]], whose author's preface is securely dated *Guāng-qǐ* 2 (886), and which presents a quite different (serious *záokǎo*) prose persona. The *Dù-yáng zá-biān* is therefore commonly dated to the *Qián-fú* 乾符 / *Guǎng-míng* 廣明 / early *Zhōng-hé* 中和 period (i.e., the years just preceding Sū's *Guāng-qǐ jìn-shì*) — c. 874–886. (The catalog date "886" thus represents the upper bound only.)

The work proceeds reign by reign, *juàn* 1 covering Dài-zōng (763–779) and Dé-zōng (779–805); *juàn* 2 Shùn-zōng (805), Xiàn-zōng (805–820), Mù-zōng (820–824), Jìng-zōng (824–826), and Wén-zōng (826–840); *juàn* 3 Wǔ-zōng (840–846), Xuān-zōng (846–859), and Yì-zōng (859–873). Each emperor receives between two and six entries, each three characters long: a tributary kingdom + an object. The opening entries on Dài-zōng include the famous *Jiǔ-huā qiú* 九花虬 — the "nine-flower coiling-dragon" horse, nine *cùn* at the forehead, with horse-coat curling like fish-scales, presented by the Fàn-yáng *jié-dù-shǐ* Lǐ Dé-shān 李德山 — which Emperor Dài bestowed on Guō Zǐ-yí 郭子儀 along with a purple-jade riding-crop after Guō's recovery of the capital. Other tribute-objects include perfumed silks from *Wū-míng-guó*, fire-resistant *huǒ-wán* 火浣 cloth, miniature mechanical theatres, glow-in-the-dark precious-stone bowls, dwarf horses of *Jū-mí-guó*, and a "*tóng-zǐ ya* 桐子鴉" — a mechanical raven of *tóng*-wood that flew on command. The collection is the canonical source for the late-Táng image of imperial luxury, and its inventory of impossible tributary objects became a stock-in-trade of Sòng *cí* and *fù* — Sū Shì, Huáng Tíng-jiān, Zhōu Mì, and many others mine it for ornament.

The historical reliability of the work is, as the *Sìkù* compilers state, low. The supposed tributary kingdoms — *Wén-dān* (= the Khmer Wén-dān? possibly), *Hē-líng* (Java?), *Dōu-lí* (possibly *Druli*, Cambodia?), *Tiáo-zhī* (the Hàn-period Antiochene Seleucid name, transparently anachronistic for the Táng), *Jì-bīn* (correctly = Kashmir, but here placed in the Western Sea) — are a mixture of dimly recalled real names and pure literary invention. The dating of the events is similarly slippery: the *Fū-yú* tribute of 841 is impossible (the kingdom no longer existed). Sū È seems to have been less interested in historiographic precision than in producing a luxurious literary-decorative inventory of late-Táng court aesthetics. Modern scholarship (Wāng Bì-cōng, Wáng Mèng-ōu, Lǐ Jiàn-guó) treats the work as essentially fictional in its tributary frame but as preserving genuine echoes of late-Táng court culture (palace-women, eunuch politics, alchemical interests of the late emperors, and so on).

The most famous single entry is the *Yāo-zǐ Cháng-cì* 妖子常賜 tale on Xuān-zōng, treating the imperial gift of a poison-vial to a courtier — an anecdote echoed in many later collections. The *Bīng-cán* 冰蠶 (Ice-Silkworm) entry on Wǔ-zōng is the source of the *bīng-cán-sī* 冰蠶絲 motif that recurs in Sòng-Yuán *cí*. The *Zhī-zú gōng-cǎo* 識足公草 (Foot-Knowing Court-Grass) entry on Wén-zōng — a grass that bows toward whoever walks past it as if recognising rank — was extensively quoted in Míng-Qīng botanical and curiosity literature.

Standard modern edition: *Dù-yáng zá-biān*, in *Táng-Sòng shǐ-liào bǐ-jì cóng-kān* 唐宋史料筆記叢刊 (Zhōng-huá shū-jú); also in *Sì-bù cóng-kān*; for an annotated text, Lǐ Jiàn-guó 李劍國, *Táng-Wǔ-dài zhì-guài chuán-qí xù-lù* 唐五代志怪傳奇敘錄 (Nán-kāi 1993), pp. 757–769.

## Other points of interest

The *Dù-yáng zá-biān* is the principal lexical and material-cultural source — alongside the *Yǒu-yáng zá-zǔ* 酉陽雜俎 — that Schafer's *Golden Peaches of Samarkand* (1963) used to reconstruct the cosmopolitan, tribute-rich court culture of the Táng. Schafer's standard methodological move is to triangulate the *Dù-yáng zá-biān*'s exotic claims against the *Táng shū Wài-guó zhuàn*, the *Tài-píng yù-lǎn*, archaeological evidence (Hé-jiā village, Famen-si, etc.), and contemporary Persian and Arab geographical literature — frequently confirming that, while the *Dù-yáng zá-biān*'s specific tributary attributions are unreliable, the underlying material classes (camphor, frankincense, asbestos cloth, mechanical automata, exotic horses, etc.) are well-attested in other sources. The book is thus a paradoxical text: factually unreliable in detail, but cumulatively a major repository of authentic late-Táng exoticism.
