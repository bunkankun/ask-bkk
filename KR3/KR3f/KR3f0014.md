# Cèliáng fǎyì 測量法義
*The Meaning of the Measurement Methods*
by [[徐光啟]] (Xú Guāngqǐ, 1562–1633, 明, *zhuàn* 撰), translating the oral teachings of [[利瑪竇]] (Matteo Ricci)

## About the work

A 1-juan trilogy (the catalog assigns single ID and single 卷-count, but the WYG actually bundles three independent works under the *Cèliáng fǎyì* heading): **(I) *Cèliáng fǎyì* 測量法義** (1 *juàn*) — the principal work, an exposition of Western trigonometric measurement theory using the *jǔdù* 矩度 (graduated quadrant) instrument; **(II) *Cèliáng yìtóng* 測量異同** (1 *juàn*) — a comparison of Western measurement methods with the Chinese *jiǔzhāng gōugǔ* 九章勾股 (right-triangle) tradition of the *Jiǔzhāng suànshù* 九章算術; **(III) *Gōugǔ yì* 勾股義** (1 *juàn*) — an exposition of the underlying right-triangle theory. All three are by Xú Guāngqǐ, presenting in Chinese the mathematical-trigonometric methods orally taught him by Matteo Ricci. Composition began in *dīngwèi* (1607), immediately following the completion of the Ricci-Xú translation of the first six books of Euclid's *Elements* (the *Jǐhé yuánběn* 幾何原本); the works are designed as practical applications of the new Euclidean geometry to surveying, height-and-distance measurement, and the foundational right-triangle relations. Xú's preface explicitly states that Ricci had been "*translating various measurement methods for ten years already*" by 1607, but that "*to attach meaning (* yì *) to the method (* fǎ *)*" — i.e. to give explanatory geometric proofs rather than mere computational recipes — became possible only after the completion of the Euclid translation. The trilogy is the foundational application of European Euclidean geometry to the Chinese surveying-and-measurement tradition, the second-generation work that turned the abstract Euclid into practical instrument-of-state.

### Tiyao

[Sub-classification: 子部, *Tiānwén suànfǎ* class 1, *tuībù* sub-category. Edition: WYG.]

Respectfully examined: *Cèliáng fǎyì* 1 *juàn*, *Cèliáng yìtóng* 1 *juàn*, *Gōugǔ yì* 1 *juàn* — by Xú Guāngqǐ of the Míng. The first *juàn* extends what Lì Mǎdòu [Ricci] translated, in order to clarify the meaning of right-triangle measurement: first the construction of the instrument — the instrument is what the *Zhōubì* calls the *jǔ* (*Try-square* — graduated quadrant); next discusses shadows: shadows have *upright* (*dǎo*) and *inverted* (*zhèng*), namely what the *Zhōubì* calls the *yǎngjǔ* (upward-square), *fùjǔ* (downward-square), and *wòjǔ* (lying-square); next sets out fifteen propositions, in order to clarify the methods of measuring-and-observing height, depth, breadth, and distance — namely what the *Zhōubì* calls *zhīgāo* (knowing height), *zhīyuǎn* (knowing distance), and *zhīshēn* (knowing depth).

The next *juàn* takes the ancient method [as preserved in] the *Jiǔzhāng* (Nine Chapters on Mathematical Procedures)' *gōugǔ* (right-triangle) measurements, and compares them with the new method to demonstrate their differences-and-similarities — in order to clarify that, although the ancient method of measurement is comprehensive, its underlying *meaning* is hidden. However, measurement is only one branch of *gōugǔ*; therefore in the third *juàn* he treats specifically the meaning of *gōugǔ*.

The preface cites the *Zhōubì* in order to clarify the source from which the establishment-of-method derives — and the rooting of the Western technique in this [Chinese tradition] is also implicitly visible.

His statement that Lǐ Yě 李冶 elaborated the right-triangle method in the *Cèyuán hǎijìng* 測圓海鏡 [does not show] understanding of the book's purport; he further says he "*wished to expound its meaning but had not had leisure*" — but this means he had not [in fact] understood the *lì tiānyuányī* 立天元一 method, and was being a fancy-sayer. The ancient *lì tiānyuányī* method *is* the Western *jiègēnfāng* 借根方 method [algebra]. By this time the Westerners had been arriving for some years, and yet of [Lǐ] Yě's book they still could not obtain its understanding. We can conclude that the *jiègēnfāng* method must have come out subsequently [to the *Cèliáng* trilogy].

The three *juàn*'s sequence is roughly thus, and their intent is in every case to illuminate the use of the *Jǐhé yuánběn* (Euclid's *Elements*). For the ancient method rarely speaks of [a problem's] meaning — when it does, it [merely] explains [each] problem [in turn]. Of the European learning, [it had] in earlier times [the figure] Ōujǐlǐdé 歐几里得 [Euclid], who according to the principles of triangles, squares, and circles propounded the various categories' [geometric] reasoning, composing a book in 13 *juàn* called the *Jǐhé yuánběn* (later Lì Mǎdòu's teacher [Christopher Clavius] *Dīngshì* added a continuation in 2 *juàn*, total 15 *juàn*). From this onward, all who studied calculation must necessarily first habituate themselves in his book. As for explaining the meaning of some method: when it is the same as something in the *Jǐhé yuánběn*, [the writer] only notes "see *Jǐhé yuánběn*, *juàn* such, section such", and does not raise his words [again]; only what the *Jǐhé yuánběn* cannot reach does he [then] explain. This is the European-learning's protocol.

Guāngqǐ, having translated with Lì Mǎdòu the first six *juàn* of the *Jǐhé yuánběn*, and wishing to use this book according to its protocol, therefore made this [trilogy] to set out the example.

His *Cèliáng fǎyì* preface says: "*To establish meaning in the method (*fǎ ér jì zhī yì yě*) — this began from the year *dīngwèi* [1607]. Why was it deferred [until then]? At that time the six *juàn* of the *Jǐhé yuánběn* had only just been finished. Reaching this point, [the meaning] could be transmitted*". Hence one can know the intent of his composing the book.

#### Original preface (Xú Guāngqǐ, 1607–1608)

*Cèliáng fǎyì*: Master Xī-Tài's [Ricci's] translation of the various measurement methods has [now been ongoing for] ten years. To attach meaning (*yì*) to method (*fǎ*) — this began from the *dīngwèi* year [1607]. Why was it deferred [until then]? At that time the six *juàn* of the *Jǐhé yuánběn* had only just been finished. Reaching this [moment], one could thereafter transmit its meaning.

This method, with the *Zhōubì*-and-*Jiǔzhāng*'s *gōugǔ cèwàng* (right-triangle measurement-and-observation) — [is it] different or [is it] not? Not different. If not different, what is its value? Also valuable for its meaning. Liú Huī, Shěn Cúnzhōng [Shěn Kuò], and that current — all once spoke of measurement-and-observation, [but] could only explain a single gnomon, [they] could not explain double gnomons; spoke of the great-and-small *gōugǔ* being mutually-derivable on the [premise that] the small *gǔ*-greater *gōu* and the small *gōu*-greater *gǔ*, the two-contained-areas, are equal — but did not say *why* they must be equal in order to be mutually-derivable. Just like Master Xī-Tài before [the year] *dīngwèi*. Why so? Without anything to serve as their *foundation*. Without anything to serve as their *foundation* — not only could the various gentlemen not say it, even Lì Shǒu [the legendary calendrical official] and Shāng Gāo could not say it. The *Zhōubì* does not speak of *foundation*? — not so: *within* its foundation there is yet another foundation. Without exhaustively explicating the *Jǐhé yuánběn*, one cannot stop. The *Yuánběn*'s capability for use is like this — and not yet exhausted. It is the *xī* [river-mouse] at the river, and the *lí* [conch] at the sea.

Why has [my work] taken these [three small problems] first? Because numbers are easy to see; small numbers are easy to explain. To broaden the technique and use it to govern water and govern fields — that benefit is great, that task is urgent. Therefore I take these first; subsequently there will be those who narrate, those who originate, who use it on a hundred-and-thousand-and-ten-thousand topics. As yet, [it is still] like drinking-from-the-river-with-a-spoon-from-the-sea — not yet exhausted. This is the *meaning* of the *Yuánběn*.

### Abstract

Composition window: 1607 (Wànlì 35, *dīngwèi* — the year Xú Guāngqǐ states for the work's beginning) – 1608 (the work was certainly complete by Xú's appointment to Tianjin agricultural duty in 1608, after which his collaboration with Ricci took different forms). The trilogy is the immediate sequel to the Ricci-Xú translation of the first six books of Euclid's *Elements* (*Jǐhé yuánběn* 幾何原本, completed *bǐngwǔ* 1606 and printed *dīngwèi* 1607).

The trilogy is foundational for two distinct reasons:

(a) **Practical demonstration of Euclid's utility**: as Xú's preface and the 提要 both emphasize, the works are designed as the demonstration of *use* (*yòng* 用) for the abstract geometric *meaning* (*yì* 義) just made available in the *Jǐhé yuánběn*. By taking the relatively elementary Chinese *gōugǔ* and *cèwàng* traditions and re-grounding them in Euclidean proof, Xú demonstrated that the new Western mathematics was not merely abstractly elegant but practically applicable to the surveying-and-measurement tasks that constituted the principal applied-mathematical work of late-imperial Chinese government. The 提要's comment that "*although the ancient method of measurement is comprehensive, its underlying meaning is hidden*" is the standard late-imperial Chinese verdict on the difference between Chinese mathematical-procedural tradition and post-Euclidean European mathematical method: the procedures are comparable, but the *axiomatic-deductive justification* that distinguishes proof from recipe is novel.

(b) **The *jiègēnfāng* / *tiānyuányī* identification**: the 提要's important historical-mathematical note — that the ancient Chinese *lì tiānyuányī* 立天元一 method (the polynomial-equation method developed by Lǐ Yě 李冶 in the 13th-century *Cèyuán hǎijìng* 測圓海鏡) is the same as the Western *jiègēnfāng* 借根方 method (algebra, transmitted to China later in the 17th century by Antoine Thomas, S.J., and integrated into the Kāngxī-period *Shùlǐ jīngyùn*) — is one of the foundational claims of the late-imperial "*Western methods originate from the East*" (*Xīfǎ Zhōngyuán* 西法中源) historiographical tradition. The 提要 is critical of Xú: had Xú properly understood Lǐ Yě's *tiānyuányī*, he would have recognized that the Western Jesuits had nothing essentially new to teach in this domain. The criticism is mathematically dubious (post-Cardano-Bombelli European algebra was substantially more advanced than Lǐ Yě's *tiānyuányī* in handling general polynomial equations) but historically revealing of late-Qīng anti-Western intellectual posture even within the broadly pro-Jesuit *Sìkù* synthesis.

The 提要's note on Christopher Clavius's continuation of Euclid (*Dīngshì xù wéi èr juàn, gòng shíwǔ juàn* "Master Dīng [Clavius] continued [Euclid] in two more *juàn*, total fifteen *juàn*") is also notable: it reflects the *Sìkù* editors' awareness that the Ricci-Xú translation went only as far as Book VI of Euclid (the canonical thirteen books plus Clavius's two-book continuation = fifteen books) and stopped there. The "rest of Euclid" was not translated into Chinese until Alexander Wylie's collaboration with Lǐ Shànlán 李善蘭 in the *Xīnjiāng yīshū* 新疆譯書 of 1857.

The methodological *credo* attributed to "the European-learning's protocol" — that one need not re-explain a result already established in Euclid but only refer to it by *juàn* and *jié* — is a striking late-Qīng recognition of the *Jǐhé yuánběn*'s status as foundational reference work in the Western mathematical tradition. The 提要's editor was effectively articulating the modern conception of *axiomatic foundation* as the Westernscientific-distinctive feature.

For Xú Guāngqǐ's biography and broader intellectual program (Catholic conversion, agricultural reform, 1611-and-1629 calendar-reform memorials, the *Chóngzhēn lìshū* directorship), see [[徐光啟]]. For the broader Jesuit-Chinese intellectual collaboration, see [[利瑪竇]].

## Other points of interest

The 提要's *Xīfǎ Zhōngyuán* (Western methods originate from the East) move on the *jiègēnfāng* / *tiānyuányī* identification deserves comparison with the parallel move on the *Zhōubì* and the European astronomy in [[KR3f0001]]: both are characteristic Qián-lóng-period assertions of Chinese intellectual priority, framed as a nuanced acknowledgment that the Westerners brought refinements but not foundational novelty. The historical-mathematical accuracy of these claims varies considerably (the *Zhōubì*-Western correlation is largely conventional; the *tiānyuányī*-algebra correlation is partly genuine but substantially overstated).

Xú Guāngqǐ's preface's vivid metaphors — Euclid's foundational character likened to the river-mouse drinking from the river and the conch from the sea (i.e. inexhaustible) — and his explicit identification of the social-utilitarian motive ("*to govern water and govern fields*") for prioritizing surveying-applications, are characteristic of his pragmatic intellectual style and his self-conception as an applied mathematician working for the public good. The phrase "*to govern water and govern fields*" foreshadows his mature program (the *Tàixī shuǐfǎ* with de Ursis, 1612; the *Nóng zhèng quán shū*, posthumous 1639) of applied science in service of state-and-people.
