# Qiánkūn tǐyì 乾坤體義
*The Substance and Meaning of Heaven and Earth* (Treatise on the Cosmos)
by [[利瑪竇]] (Matteo Ricci, *Lì Mǎdòu*, S.J., 1552–1610, 明, *zhuàn* 撰)

## About the work

Matteo Ricci's first systematic exposition in Chinese of European cosmography and applied geometry, in 3 *juàn*. The upper and middle *juàn* present the cosmological-astronomical doctrine: a spherical Earth divided into the five climatic zones (*hánnuǎn wǔdài* 寒煖五帶) by latitude, the geocentric nine-celestial-spheres model (the *jiǔzhòng tiān* 九重天 of Aristotelian-Ptolemaic astronomy as transmitted through the Coimbra Jesuit syllabus), the four classical elements (*shuǐhuǒtǔqì* 水火土氣 — water, fire, earth, air, replacing the Chinese five-phase scheme); the geometric explanation of solar and lunar eclipses through Earth-shadow; the relative-size ratios of Sun, Moon, the planets, and the Earth; and the theory of atmospheric refraction (*yìngméng* 映蒙) to explain the apparent enlargement of the rising-and-setting solar and lunar discs. The lower *juàn* presents elementary practical geometry: line-and-area computations, plane and elliptical figures, mutual containment-and-comparison of figures, treated as a supplement to the classical Chinese *fāngtián* 方田 (square-field) tradition (*juānbǔ gǔ fāngtián zhī suǒ wèijí* 補古方田之所未及).

The 提要 — written from the perspective of the Qīng synthetic mathematical tradition that had absorbed and refined the Jesuit material — pronounces an unusually positive verdict: "*its words are all verified by actual observation; its methods all carry through completely* (*qí yán jiē yàn zhū shícè, qí fǎ jiē jù dé biàntōng* 其言皆騐諸實測, 其法皆具得變通)". The editors explicitly trace the genealogy of the Kāngxī-Qiánlóng *imperial Shùlǐ jīngyùn* 數理精藴 back through Xú Guāngqǐ's mid-17th-century reform to this very text — *shì shū gù yì dàlù zhī chuílún yǐ* 是書固亦大輅之椎輪矣 ("this book is indeed the unhewn-spoked wheel of the great Imperial Carriage" — the foundational pre-form of the eventual achievement).

### Tiyao

[Sub-classification: 子部, *Tiānwén suànfǎ* class 1, *tuībù* sub-category. Edition: WYG.]

Respectfully examined: *Qiánkūn tǐyì*, 3 *juàn*, by Lì Mǎdòu of the Míng. Lì Mǎdòu was a Western man; in the Wànlì period he sailed to Guǎngdōng — this is the beginning of Western methods entering China. Lì Mǎdòu had penetrated both Chinese and Western literatures; therefore all the books he authored are in Chinese characters and Chinese language, requiring no second translation.

The book's upper and middle *juàn* both treat heavenly phenomena: dividing the inhabited regions of cold-and-warmth into the five [climatic] zones, taking sun, moon, and stars in heaven as nine layers, taking water, fire, earth, and air (*qì*) as the four great primary phases (*sì dà yuánxíng* 四大元行); using sun, moon, and earth-shadow — the three things — to determine the eclipse-magnitudes; reaching as far as the constant-stars and the Seven Regulators having ratios-of-doubling [in size] with the earth, and sun-and-moon rising-and-setting each having reflection-and-mist (*yìngméng* — atmospheric refraction); much disclosing what earlier men did not disclose. Where his many-sided rare similes [are deployed, they] are also all winding-yet-detailed-and-clear.

The lower *juàn* all treats arithmetic — taking edge-lines and surface-areas, plane-circles and elliptical-circles, mutually containing-and-comparing, supplementing what the ancient *Fāngtián* did not reach — making a foundation for present-day line, surface, and solid [computation]. Although the fascicles are not many, his words are all verified by actual measurement; his methods all carry through completely. What is called "diction concise but meaning complete" — Our Dynasty's [imperial] *Shùlǐ jīngyùn* mostly takes its arguments from [this work] and develops them further. At the time of the late-Míng calendrical-method's confusion, the Prince-Heir of Zhèng [Zhū] Zàiyù, Xíng Yúnlù, and other men all forcefully refuted the [established] errors, but their learning was not yet sufficient to triumph. Only after Xú Guāngqǐ and others changed to using the new methods did [Chinese mathematical-astronomical understanding] gradually pass from sparse to refined; and in Our Dynasty [the Qīng] [study] has been further deepened, only now beginning to exhaust the subtle. Then this book is indeed the unhewn-spoked wheel of the Great Imperial Carriage.

Respectfully collated, *Qiánlóng* 46, ninth month [October 1781].

Chief Compilers: (subject) Jì Yún 紀昀, (subject) Lù Xíxióng 陸錫熊, (subject) Sūn Shìyì 孫士毅. Chief Collator: (subject) Lù Fèichí 陸費墀.

### Abstract

Composition window: 1605 (the year by which Ricci had completed his major mathematical-cosmographic Chinese publications and was working on integrating the European geometry-and-cosmology curriculum for Chinese readers — *Tiānzhǔ shíyì* had appeared 1603, *Èrshíwǔ yán* in 1605) – 1610 (Ricci's death). The work appears to have circulated in manuscript late in his life; printed editions in continuous circulation from the Chóngzhēn period onward. The *Qiánkūn tǐyì*'s underlying European source is the standard Coimbra-Jesuit *Sphaera mundi* tradition derived from Sacrobosco's *Tractatus de sphaera* with the *Aristotelis Stagiritae De caelo et mundo* commentary tradition, transmitted through Christopher Clavius's lectures at the Roman College where Ricci studied in the 1570s. The geometric material in the lower *juàn* is preparatory work in the line of Clavius's geometry textbooks, anticipating the more systematic *Jǐhé yuánběn* 幾何原本 (Ricci-Xú Guāngqǐ Euclid translation) of 1607.

The work is foundational in two distinct ways:

(a) **The geocentric nine-spheres model**: the *Qiánkūn tǐyì*'s presentation of the *jiǔzhòng tiān* (nine concentric celestial spheres) was the first connected Chinese-language exposition of the Aristotelian-Ptolemaic geocentric universe. Although later Jesuit astronomers (notably Adam Schall von Bell and Ferdinand Verbiest) would shift to the modified Tycho-Brahe geo-heliocentric system in their late-Míng / early-Qīng calendrical writings, the *Qiánkūn tǐyì*'s geocentric framework was the entry point — and the framework that the *Sìkù* editors a century-and-a-half later still saw as foundational.

(b) **The five climatic zones and round-Earth doctrine**: the work's elementary geographic exposition — five latitudinal zones, antipodal symmetry, atmospheric refraction at the horizon — together with the parallel cartographic project of the *Kūnyú wànguó quántú* 坤輿萬國全圖 (1602), introduced the round-Earth doctrine to mainstream Chinese intellectual discourse. The *Sìkù* editors' position that "Chinese mathematical-astronomical understanding gradually passed from sparse to refined" via Ricci's foundation is one of the most explicit pieces of late-imperial Chinese acknowledgment of intellectual debt to European science.

The 提要's narrative arc — late-Míng calendrical breakdown → Zhū Zàiyù and Xíng Yúnlù's diagnosis but limited remedy → Ricci's foundational supply of new tools → Xú Guāngqǐ's mid-century reform → the Kāngxī-Qiánlóng *Shùlǐ jīngyùn* synthesis as completion — is the standard late-imperial Chinese account of the period 1580–1750 in mathematical astronomy, and the *Qiánkūn tǐyì* is the indispensable intermediary text in this story.

The work's status as the first significant Western-mathematical text to be entered into the Sìkù — preceding even the *Jǐhé yuánběn* itself in the Sìkù arrangement — gives it a peculiar canonical importance. For Ricci's biography, mission method, and the broader Jesuit-Chinese intellectual project, see the [[利瑪竇]] person note.

## Other points of interest

The 提要's editorial emphasis on Ricci's dual literary competence — "*Lì Mǎdòu had penetrated both Chinese and Western literatures; therefore all the books he authored are in Chinese characters and Chinese language, requiring no second translation*" — is a striking late-Qīng acknowledgment of the unusual character of the Ricci texts in the Chinese canonical record: they are the only major *Sìkù* entries authored *de novo* in literary Chinese by a foreigner.

The 提要's complete silence on the *religious* purpose of Ricci's work — situating the *Qiánkūn tǐyì* purely in the *Tiānwén suànfǎ* (mathematical astronomy) compartment, with no mention of his Christian mission — is consistent with the Qián-lóng-period editorial policy of bracketing Catholic-doctrinal content while preserving the scientific output. (Ricci's overtly theological *Tiānzhǔ shíyì* 天主實義 was placed not in the *Sìkù* but in the *Cúnmù* 存目 list of titles-known-but-not-included.)
