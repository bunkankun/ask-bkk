# Tiānwèn lüè 天問畧
*Brief Treatise on Heavenly Questions* (Catechism of Astronomy)
by [[陽瑪諾]] (Manuel Dias Jr., S.J., *Yáng Mǎnuò*, 1574–1659, 明, *zhuàn* 撰)

## About the work

Manuel Dias's 1-*juàn* question-and-answer (*wèndá* 問答) introduction to the European cosmographic-astronomical curriculum, presented in fifteen progressively-developed dialogues, written in Chinese in 1615 (Wànlì *yǐmǎo*). The work expounds: (1) the twelve-celestial-spheres cosmology (*shíèr zhòng tiān* 十二重天) — extending the nine spheres of Matteo Ricci's [[KR3f0009]] *Qiánkūn tǐyì* by adding the tenth and eleventh "primum mobile" spheres and the twelfth, the immobile *empyreum* described in the original preface as the dwelling-place of God and the saints; (2) the placement of the Seven Regulators (sun, moon, five planets) within the spheres; (3) the geometry of solar position and the seasons; (4) the geometry of variable day-and-night length at different latitudes; (5) the geometry of solar and lunar eclipses; (6) the cause of the Earth's shadow as ground of lunar eclipse; (7) the theory of atmospheric refraction (*méngqì* 矇氣 / *yìngyàng* 映漾) explaining the apparent enlargement of the rising-and-setting sun; (8) the theory of optical lingering ("*lingering light*" *liúguāng* 留光). The text concludes with a tabular *méngyǐng kèfēn biǎo* 矇影刻分表 (atmospheric-refraction time-table) and a detailed gnomonical exposition of conjunction, opposition, and eclipse geometry. The work is structurally companion to [[KR3f0010]] *Biǎo dù shuō* — "*forming the inner-and-outer of one another's books*" (*hù wéi biǎolǐ zhī shū* 互為表裏之書) per the Sìkù 提要 — and presupposes its gnomon-shadow exposition.

The 提要 records an unusually frank editorial intervention by the Sìkù editors. The original preface by Yáng Mǎnuò "*set aside its real techniques and instead vigorously praised the so-called Lord-of-Heaven [Tiānzhǔ — God], and further claimed that the immobile twelfth heaven is the dwelling of all the saints and the location of the heavenly hall, and that only those who believe-and-serve the Lord-of-Heaven can ascend to it, in order to entice the lower-foolish [common people]*". The editors continue: "*[the author] aimed to use the verifiable nature of [astronomical] computation to prove the deceit of [the doctrines of] the Lord-of-Heaven and the heavenly hall — his intent is extraordinarily perverse-and-deceitful (* qí yòngyì jí wéi guǐjué *其用意極為詭譎). However, his verification of celestial phenomena is in fact superior to the ancient methods. Now we have set aside his absurd, deception-selling sayings, and have taken only his refined and well-grounded methods. We have deleted the original preface to avoid misleading the listeners. As for the passages within the body that touch on the false-and-erroneous, were we to expurgate them the textual sense would not connect — so we have provisionally retained the old [text] while refuting his heterodox doctrines as above*". The catalog entry preserves the work for its science but explicitly disowns its theology: a paradigmatic case of the late-Qīng editorial separation of "Western technical learning" from "Western religion".

### Abstract

Composition: 1615 (Wànlì *yǐmǎo*), the date stated by the 提要. Manuel Dias the Younger had been at the Beijing residence for four years and was working in close consultation with Sabatino de Ursis (whose *Jiǎnpíng yí shuō* of 1611 [[KR3f0011]] and *Biǎo dù shuō* of 1614 [[KR3f0010]] had immediately preceded). The work fits as the third-in-sequence of the Beijing-residence post-Ricci cosmographic primers, completing the curriculum: instrument-and-stereographic-projection (de Ursis 1611), gnomon-shadow-and-elementary-geometry (de Ursis 1614), and now the synthetic exposition of celestial mechanics with eclipse and refraction theory (Dias 1615). After 1616 the Nánjīng anti-Christian persecution disrupted further large-scale Jesuit publication for some years; the *Tiānwèn lüè* thus marks the natural endpoint of the immediate post-Ricci pedagogical project.

Two interpretive themes deserve emphasis. First, the work's structural position as a *catechism* (in the literal sense — *wèndá* 問答) is intentional. The Catholic catechetical tradition (Trent's *Catechismus Romanus* of 1566 and its derivative national-and-language adaptations) was Dias's structural model. By presenting astronomy in catechetical question-and-answer form, Dias made the natural-philosophical content as portable, memorizable, and devotionally-oriented as the religious catechism — an explicit pedagogical bridge between the natural and the supernatural. The Sìkù editors' irritation at the original preface ("*his intent is extraordinarily perverse-and-deceitful*") reflects their accurate perception that this was the work's deepest design.

Second, the *Sìkù* editorial intervention here is among the most explicit anywhere in the Qián-lóng-period editing of the Western collection. The editors' candor about *what* they deleted (the original preface entirely) and *why* (to avoid "*misleading the listeners*", *yíng tīng* 熒聴) is unusual; their decision to retain the body text against their own theological objections (because deletion would "*break the textual sense*") reflects a genuine scholarly compromise. The result is a textual artifact in which the work's argumentative structure is preserved but its devotional framing is removed — a paradigm of the late-Qīng "Western learning, Chinese essence" (*Zhōngtǐ Xīyòng* 中體西用) attitude *avant la lettre*.

Technical content: the work's most influential single contribution is the systematic introduction to Chinese readers of (a) the Aristotelian theory of atmospheric refraction (*méngqì* 矇氣, the *vapor humidus* of Latin sources) as the cause of the apparent enlargement of the rising-and-setting sun and moon — a theory the work attributes to "investigations of our country's calendrical scholars" and that became standard in Qīng astronomical handbooks; (b) the *liúguāng* 留光 ("lingering light") theory explaining the *crepusculum* (twilight) duration through the persistence of refracted-and-scattered solar light below the horizon down to 18°; (c) the empirical observation that the planet Venus and Mercury, when in conjunction with the sun, appear as black dots on the solar disk — Dias notes this as common knowledge among "*our country's calendrical specialists*". This last is a startling early notice of what would later be called the planetary transits of Venus and Mercury; Dias does not relate it to its later European observational tradition (Galileo's first observation of a Mercury transit was the same decade, but the European context probably did not reach Dias before he wrote).

The geographic-tabular content is also substantial: the work's tables of day-length and twilight duration for fifteen Míng provinces (Beijing, Nánjīng, Shāndōng, Shānxī, Shǎnxī, Hénán, Zhèjiāng, Jiāngxī, Húguǎng, Sìchuān, Fújiàn, Guǎngdōng, Guǎngxī, Yúnnán, Guìzhōu) are the most extensive set of latitude-based astronomical data in the Wànlì-period Jesuit corpus, and were widely consulted into the early Qīng.

For Manuel Dias's biography and broader career, see the [[陽瑪諾]] person note. For the broader Jesuit-Chinese astronomical project, see [[利瑪竇]] and [[熊三拔]].

## Other points of interest

The 提要's editorial confession is the most explicit known statement of Qián-lóng-period editorial criteria for Jesuit texts: science preserved, theology excised, the textual seams left visible "*so as to refute his heterodox doctrines*". Read alongside the 提要 of [[KR3f0009]] *Qiánkūn tǐyì* (which silently bracketed the same questions about Ricci's religious purposes), this 提要 documents the late-Qīng editorial system's evolution: where Ricci's foundational text was treated by silence, Dias's more aggressive theological framing required active intervention.

Manuel Dias's identification with the immobile-twelfth-heaven Empyrean as the dwelling of God and the saints — a doctrine native to medieval Catholic cosmology (Dante's *Paradiso* canto 27 onward, formalized by the Council of Vienne 1311) — was the precise form in which late-medieval European Aristotelian-Ptolemaic astronomy articulated its theological framework. Dias's transmission of this synthesis into Chinese in 1615 brought the Catholic medieval cosmographic-theological inheritance into the Wànlì philosophical conversation in concentrated form; the 提要's reaction shows how easily this inheritance could be flagged as foreign.
