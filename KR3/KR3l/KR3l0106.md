# Dùyáng zábiān 杜陽雜編
*Miscellaneous Compilation from Du-yang*
by [[蘇鶚]] (撰)

## About the work

A three-*juàn* late-Táng anecdotal-anomaly compilation by Sū È 蘇鶚 (*zì* Déxiáng 德祥, *fl.* late 9th c.; *jìnshì* of the *Guāngqǐ* 光啟 reign-period, 885–888), spanning the reigns of *ten emperors* — Dàizōng 代宗 (763) through Yìzōng 懿宗 (873–4) — and titled after his native village Dùyáng 杜陽 in Wǔgōng 武功 (modern Shǎnxī). Each entry takes a three-character heading and centres on (i) an extraordinary tribute-object (*gòngwù* 貢物) supposedly presented to the Táng court by some named outer-realm kingdom, (ii) a court anecdote about the emperor's reception of it, and (iii) the moral or aesthetic point Sū draws. The book is the principal repository for the fabulous tribute-culture of the late Táng — fragrant silks, fire-resistant jades, glowing pearls, automata, dwarf horses, and so on — and was already by the Sòng a major mine for *lèishū* compilers and *cí*-poets. The *Sìkù* compilers candidly note that many of the supposed tributary states are unattested in the *Táng shū* and that the geographical and chronological claims are frequently impossible (e.g., the Fūyú 夫餘 kingdom, long absorbed into Bóhǎi by the 9th c., is described as paying tribute in 841), but they retain it for its rich vocabulary and stylistic value — "the *xiǎoshuō* writer who wins by literary colour" (*xiǎoshuōjiā zhī yǐ wéncǎi shèng zhě* 小說家之以文采勝者).

### Tiyao

Your servants report: *Dùyáng zábiān* in 3 *juàn*. The Táng Sū È 蘇鶚 *zhuàn*. È is the author of the *Yǎnyì* 演義, already separately catalogued. This compilation covers from Dàizōng *Guǎngdé* 1 (763) at the top down to Yìzōng *Xiántōng* 14 (873) — the affairs of ten reigns. All entries take three-character headings. Within, the descriptions of strange crafts and precious objects are of the kind that strays beyond the standard. In general, it descends from Wáng Jiā's 王嘉 *Shíyí jì* 拾遺記 and Guō Zǐhéng's 郭子橫 *Dòngmíng jì* 洞冥記 — though it always cites the source-person to substantiate, this is yet only the way colloquial talk turns into bright pigment. The names it gives — *Rìlín* 日林, *Dàlín* 大林, *Wéndān* 文單, *Wūmíng* 烏明, *Jūmí* 拘弭, *Dàzhěn* 大軫, *Nánchāng* 南昌, *Zhèdōng* 淛東, *Tiáozhī* 條支, *Guǐgǔ* 鬼谷, *Hēlíng* 訶陵, *Dōulí* 兜離 (claiming each as the donor of a certain object in a certain year) — the *Táng shū* *Wàiguó zhuàn* gives none of these names, nor do the various imperial annals carry the supposed events. To take one example: the Fūyú 夫餘 kingdom was long since absorbed by Bóhǎi 渤海's Dà 大 clan, yet it claims that in Wǔzōng *Huìchāng* 1 (841) Fūyú came to pay tribute. As for Jìbīn 罽賓 — which borders on the Cōnglǐng [Pamirs], as both the *Hàn shū* and the *Táng shū* state plainly — it is here said to lie in the Western Sea. These are clearly egregious. Yet its elaborate, ornate display is constantly mined for material by poets and *fù*-writers: it is the *xiǎoshuō* writer who wins by literary colour. The reader sucks in its sweet petals and so forgets its exaggerations; even today the citations continue. The name *Dùyáng zábiān*: Cháo Gōngwǔ's 晁公武 *Dúshū zhì* says that È lived at Dùyáng of Wǔgōng — he therefore named his book after the place.

Respectfully checked, *Qiánlóng* 46 (1781), 9th month. Chief Compilers: Jì Yún 紀昀, Lù Xīxióng 陸錫熊, Sūn Shìyì 孫士毅. Chief Collator: Lù Fèichí 陸費墀.

### Abstract

The composition window adopted here is 874–886. The lower bound is fixed by the latest event the book covers — Yìzōng *Xiántōng* 14 (873), the year of Yìzōng's death — which must have been written at the earliest in 874; the upper bound is fixed by Sū È's other surviving work, the *Sūshì yǎnyì* 蘇氏演義 [[KR3j0028]], whose author's preface is securely dated *Guāngqǐ* 2 (886), and which presents a quite different (serious *záokǎo*) prose persona. The *Dùyáng zábiān* is therefore commonly dated to the *Qiánfú* 乾符 / *Guǎngmíng* 廣明 / early *Zhōnghé* 中和 period (i.e., the years just preceding Sū's *Guāngqǐ jìnshì*) — c. 874–886. (The catalog date "886" thus represents the upper bound only.)

The work proceeds reign by reign, *juàn* 1 covering Dàizōng (763–779) and Dézōng (779–805); *juàn* 2 Shùnzōng (805), Xiànzōng (805–820), Mùzōng (820–824), Jìngzōng (824–826), and Wénzōng (826–840); *juàn* 3 Wǔzōng (840–846), Xuānzōng (846–859), and Yìzōng (859–873). Each emperor receives between two and six entries, each three characters long: a tributary kingdom + an object. The opening entries on Dàizōng include the famous *Jiǔhuā qiú* 九花虬 — the "nine-flower coiling-dragon" horse, nine *cùn* at the forehead, with horse-coat curling like fish-scales, presented by the Fànyáng *jiédùshǐ* Lǐ Déshān 李德山 — which Emperor Dài bestowed on Guō Zǐyí 郭子儀 along with a purple-jade riding-crop after Guō's recovery of the capital. Other tribute-objects include perfumed silks from *Wūmíngguó*, fire-resistant *huǒwán* 火浣 cloth, miniature mechanical theatres, glow-in-the-dark precious-stone bowls, dwarf horses of *Jūmíguó*, and a "*tóngzǐ ya* 桐子鴉" — a mechanical raven of *tóng*-wood that flew on command. The collection is the canonical source for the late-Táng image of imperial luxury, and its inventory of impossible tributary objects became a stock-in-trade of Sòng *cí* and *fù* — Sū Shì, Huáng Tíngjiān, Zhōu Mì, and many others mine it for ornament.

The historical reliability of the work is, as the *Sìkù* compilers state, low. The supposed tributary kingdoms — *Wéndān* (= the Khmer Wéndān? possibly), *Hēlíng* (Java?), *Dōulí* (possibly *Druli*, Cambodia?), *Tiáozhī* (the Hàn-period Antiochene Seleucid name, transparently anachronistic for the Táng), *Jìbīn* (correctly = Kashmir, but here placed in the Western Sea) — are a mixture of dimly recalled real names and pure literary invention. The dating of the events is similarly slippery: the *Fūyú* tribute of 841 is impossible (the kingdom no longer existed). Sū È seems to have been less interested in historiographic precision than in producing a luxurious literary-decorative inventory of late-Táng court aesthetics. Modern scholarship (Wāng Bìcōng, Wáng Mèngōu, Lǐ Jiànguó) treats the work as essentially fictional in its tributary frame but as preserving genuine echoes of late-Táng court culture (palace-women, eunuch politics, alchemical interests of the late emperors, and so on).

The most famous single entry is the *Yāozǐ Chángcì* 妖子常賜 tale on Xuānzōng, treating the imperial gift of a poison-vial to a courtier — an anecdote echoed in many later collections. The *Bīngcán* 冰蠶 (Ice-Silkworm) entry on Wǔzōng is the source of the *bīngcánsī* 冰蠶絲 motif that recurs in Sòng-Yuán *cí*. The *Zhīzú gōngcǎo* 識足公草 (Foot-Knowing Court-Grass) entry on Wénzōng — a grass that bows toward whoever walks past it as if recognising rank — was extensively quoted in Míng-Qīng botanical and curiosity literature.

Standard modern edition: *Dùyáng zábiān*, in *Táng-Sòng shǐliào bǐjì cóngkān* 唐宋史料筆記叢刊 (Zhōnghuá shūjú); also in *Sìbù cóngkān*; for an annotated text, Lǐ Jiànguó 李劍國, *Táng-Wǔdài zhìguài chuánqí xùlù* 唐五代志怪傳奇敘錄 (Nánkāi 1993), pp. 757–769.

## Other points of interest

The *Dùyáng zábiān* is the principal lexical and material-cultural source — alongside the *Yǒuyáng zázǔ* 酉陽雜俎 — that Schafer's *Golden Peaches of Samarkand* (1963) used to reconstruct the cosmopolitan, tribute-rich court culture of the Táng. Schafer's standard methodological move is to triangulate the *Dùyáng zábiān*'s exotic claims against the *Táng shū Wàiguó zhuàn*, the *Tàipíng yùlǎn*, archaeological evidence (Héjiā village, Famen-si, etc.), and contemporary Persian and Arab geographical literature — frequently confirming that, while the *Dùyáng zábiān*'s specific tributary attributions are unreliable, the underlying material classes (camphor, frankincense, asbestos cloth, mechanical automata, exotic horses, etc.) are well-attested in other sources. The book is thus a paradoxical text: factually unreliable in detail, but cumulatively a major repository of authentic late-Táng exoticism.
