# Yuányě 園冶
*The Craft of Gardens*
by [[計成]] (Jì Chéng, b. 1582, fl. 1631–1635, 明, *zhuàn* 撰)

## About the work

The single most important Chinese theoretical treatise on garden design, composed by Jì Chéng 計成 (*zì* Wúpǐ 無否, *hào* Pǐdào rén 否道人, of Wújiāng 吳江 in Sūzhōu prefecture, b. 1582). The work was completed in *Chóngzhēn xīnwèi* 崇禎辛未 (1631) and published in *Chóngzhēn jiǎxū* 崇禎甲戌 (1634) with a postface by Jì then aged 53. Originally titled *Yuánmù* 園牧 (the "Garden Pastorate"), the title was changed to *Yuányě* 園冶 ("Garden Crafting") at the suggestion of Cáo Yuánfǔ 曹元甫 of Gūshú 姑孰 (i.e. Dāngtú 當塗). The treatise is in three *juàn*: *juàn* 1 covers (1) *Xìngzào lùn* 興造論 (theory of construction), and the *Xiāngdì* 相地 (site-divining), *Lìjī* 立基 (foundation-laying), *Wūyǔ* 屋宇 (buildings, with detailed elevation- and plan-illustrations of *tānggé*, *zhāi*, *guǎn*, *lóu*, *tái*, *gé*, *tíng*, *xiè*, *xuān*, *juǎn*, *guǎng*, *láng*, and seven roof-frame types) and *Zhuāngzhé* 裝折 (interior partition); *juàn* 2 is on the *Lángān* 欄桿 (balustrades) — 100 patterns; *juàn* 3 covers *Ménchuāng* 門窗 (doors and windows), *Qiángyuán* 牆垣 (walls and fences), *Pūdì* 鋪地 (paving), *Duōshān* 掇山 (rockwork composition), *Xuǎnshí* 選石 (stone selection — Tàihúshí, Kūnshān, Yíxìng, Lóngtán, Jīnlíng Qīnglóngshān, Língbì, Yīngshí etc.), and the famous closing chapter *Jièjǐng* 借景 ("borrowed scenery"). The *Jièjǐng* chapter develops the doctrine of *jiè* (borrowing) — *yuǎn jiè* 遠借 (distant borrowing), *lín jiè* 鄰借 (neighbouring borrowing), *yǎng jiè* 仰借 (looking-up borrowing), *fǔ jiè* 俯借 (looking-down borrowing), and *yìngshí ér jiè* 應時而借 (borrowing-according-to-season) — fundamental to all later Chinese garden theory.

### Prefaces

Three substantial paratexts frame the work, all preserved in the *krp-titles* transmission:

**Jì Chéng's self-preface (自序):** Jì recounts his life: in youth he was known as a painter, modelled himself on the Five-Dynasties landscape masters Guān Tóng 關仝 and Jīng Hào 荊浩, travelled in Yān 燕 and Chǔ 楚 lands; in middle age he returned to Wú 吳 and settled at Rùnzhōu 潤州 (Zhènjiāng). There local enthusiasts collected clever rocks and piled them as fake mountains among bamboo and trees; Jì found this risible, saying: "the world hears 'where there is a real thing there is a fake of it' — why not fake the real mountain-shape, instead of *fake* clenched-fist rocks?" Asked whether he could do better, Jì constructed a *bì* 壁 (wall composition) which observers immediately recognised as a *yǎnrán jiā shān* (a true fine mountain). He came to the notice of the *Jìnlíng Fāngbó* (provincial intendant) Wú Yòuyú 吳又予 of Jìnlíng, who employed him to design a 15-*mǔ* garden on the old site of Wēnxiàngfǔ 元溫相 (Yuán Tàishī's old garden) east of the city. Subsequently Wāng Shìhéng 汪士衡 (a *zhōnghàn* secretary) employed Jì at *Luánjiāng* on the west, "matched in ambition" with Wú's project. In idle time Jì drafted the design-models, calling them *Yuánmù* (the Garden Pastorate). Cáo Yuánfǔ of Gūshú, on visiting, urged Jì to retitle it *Yuányě* — "yours is opening a new path; let it be called *yě* (crafting) rather than *mù* (pastorate)." The self-preface is signed *Chóngzhēn xīnwèi zhīqiū miǎo, Pǐdào rén, xián yú Hùyětáng zhōng tí* (the autumnal *miǎo* of *Chóngzhēn xīnwèi* / 1631, Pǐdào rén, at leisure inscribed in the *Hùyětáng*).

**Yuán Yúlìng 阮于令 preface:** *Chóngzhēn jiǎxū* (1634); preserved in the cut-block edition.

**Jì Chéng's postface (卷後):** "In *Chóngzhēn jiǎxū* (1634), I am 53 *suì*. I have exhausted the dust of the world, weary of *yóuyè* (sojourning), with a small *línxià fēngqù* (taste of retirement-under-trees); long depending on garden-park, my interest with worldly affairs feels distant; only hearing the times' affairs in turbulence, the retiring heart is so. Ashamed of having no money to buy a mountain, I am willing to be a *Táoyuán xīkǒu rén* (a man of the Peach Blossom Spring stream-mouth). Sighing at this human birth's not-meeting-its-time: even Zhūgé Liàng *Wǔhóu* the Three-Kingdoms *shī* (teacher), Liánggōng (Dí Rénjié) as the *Nǚwáng* (Wǔ Zétiān)'s *xiàng* — the ancient *xiánháo* in their not-meeting-time; how much more this rustic and slow person. I write this *Yě* for my two sons *Chángshēng* and *Chángjí*, that they may have something like pears and chestnuts in their futures. So I have it carved and circulated, that it may be of use to the world."

### Abstract

The *Yuányě* is the foundation of Chinese garden theory and the single most influential premodern Chinese architectural treatise on the design of landscape gardens. Jì Chéng 計成 (b. 1582 per CBDB; death-date uncertain, with the postface establishing he was alive in 1634 aged 53; the catalog meta gives dynasty as Míng without precise dates) is one of the few documented professional garden designers in the Chinese tradition. The work was completed at the end of the Wànlì and the start of the Chóngzhēn — the same generation as the late-Míng connoisseurial compendia of Zhāng Chǒu [[KR3h0055]] and Wāng Kěyù [[KR3h0060]], and the architectural treatises of the Sòng-Yìngzàofǎshì tradition. The work is divided into theoretical exposition (the *Xìngzào lùn* of *juàn* 1, the *Jièjǐng* of *juàn* 3 close) and practical instruction with extensive engraved illustrations (building-frames, balustrade patterns, door-and-window patterns, wall patterns, rock-stacking diagrams). The work fell into relative neglect in the early Qīng (the Sìkù editors did not include it, perhaps because of Jì's association with the Wú Yòuyú garden-construction patronage network), but was rediscovered by Japanese architects (the *Yuányě* was preserved in Japan, where it influenced Edo-period garden theory), and reintroduced to Chinese scholarship in the 1930s by Yuán Yěshēng 元冶生 / Liú Dūnzhēn 劉敦楨 and others. The work's three foundational concepts — *yīn* 因 (according-with: working with the site), *jiè* 借 (borrowing: integrating outside views), and *tǐyí* 體宜 (body-and-fittingness: appropriate scale) — became the canonical vocabulary of all later Chinese garden criticism.

## Other points of interest

The *Yuányě* is the only major Chinese garden-design treatise to survive in pre-modern China complete with technical illustrations, and the only pre-modern Chinese architectural treatise to give a systematic poetics of practice. The discovery of the *Yuányě* by Japanese architectural historians in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and its retro-introduction to Chinese architectural scholarship in the 1930s, is itself a major episode in modern Sino-Japanese cultural history. The work has been the principal source for the modern restoration and reconstruction of Jiāngnán private gardens (Sūzhōu, Wúxī, Yángzhōu) from the 1950s onwards.
