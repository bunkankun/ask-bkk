# Huáng Hàn yī xué 皇漢醫學
*Imperial-Han Medicine*
by [[湯本求真]] (Yumoto Kyūshin / Tāngběn Qiúzhēn, 1876–1941, 大正・昭和)

## About the work

A three-volume early-Shōwa comprehensive defense and exposition of *Shānghán* / *Jīnguì* clinical doctrine by [[湯本求真]] Yumoto Kyūshin, the principal early-twentieth-century Japanese reviver of the *kanpō* / *kohō* tradition. Originally published in Tokyo 1927–1928 as *Kōkan igaku* 皇漢醫學, the work was translated into Chinese by Zhōu Zǐxù 周子敘 in 1930 and became one of the most influential single texts in the twentieth-century Chinese-medicine revival movement on both sides of the East China Sea.

### Abstract

The self-preface (自序), dated 昭和二年(一九二七)六月上旬 = early June 1927 (Tokyo, Tabata 田端), records Yumoto's biographical conversion narrative: born to a medical family, trained in Western medicine at the Kanazawa Medical Specialist School (graduated 1901), he practiced as a Western-medical doctor for nearly a decade before the death of his eldest daughter from dysentery in *Meiji* 43 (1910) — which he attributed to the failure of Western therapeutics — turned him toward the *kanpō* tradition under the influence of [[和田啟十郎]] Wada Keijūrō's *Igaku no tettsui* 醫界鐵椎. Eighteen years of further study followed before the publication of *Kōkan igaku*.

The work is organized in three volumes covering (1) general doctrine and pathophysiology, (2) *Shānghán* prescriptions clause-by-clause, and (3) *Jīnguì* prescriptions clause-by-clause, throughout integrating modern Western pharmacology and clinical observation with classical Chinese-Japanese medical doctrine. Its argument — that the *Shānghán*–*Jīnguì* clinical system constitutes a rigorous, evidence-based therapeutic tradition equal in standing to modern Western medicine — was a watershed for the twentieth-century revival of East Asian medicine, and the Chinese translation circulated widely in Republican-period Shanghai, where it shaped figures such as [[曹穎甫]] (Cáo Yǐngfǔ) and the Shanghai *Shānghán*-school circle.

The catalog meta gives the author as "日本· 湯本求真" with no dynasty — the correct designation is *Taishō / Shōwa* (1927–28), or in Chinese chronology *Mínguó* 民國.

## Translations and research

- *No substantial English-language translation located.*

## Other points of interest

This is one of the most influential single twentieth-century texts in East Asian medicine: it is the principal Japanese contribution to the Republican-period Chinese-medicine revival, and one of the principal sources used by Republican-era Shanghai-school *Shānghán* clinicians (including [[曹穎甫]] Cáo Yǐngfǔ — see [[KR3ef093]]).
