# Lì-tǐ lüè 歷體畧
*Brief Treatise on the Calendrical-Body* (the celestial-mechanical foundations of calendrical computation)
by [[王英明]] (Wáng Yīngmíng, fl. 1606–1612, 明, *zhuàn* 撰); diagrams supplied by Wēng Hànlín 翁漢麐 in the 1646 edition

## About the work

Wáng Yīngmíng's 3-juan introduction to the cosmological-astronomical foundations underlying calendrical computation, completed Wànlì *rén-zǐ* (1612) and printed posthumously by his son Wáng Yǎng 王懩 in Shùnzhì *bǐng-xū* (1646) with diagrams supplied by Wēng Hànlín 翁漢麐. The work consists of:

**Upper *juàn* (6 chapters)**: *Tiān-tǐ dì-xíng* 天體地形 (heaven-body and earth-form), *Èr-yào* 二曜 (the two luminaries — sun and moon), *Wǔ-wěi* 五緯 (the five planets), *Chén-cì* 辰次 (the lunar-stations and zodiacal sequence), *Kè-lòu jí-dù* 刻漏極度 (clepsydra-divisions and polar elevation), *Zá-shuō* 雜説 (miscellany).

**Middle *juàn* (3 chapters)**: *Jí-gōng* 極宫 (the polar palaces — the asterisms around the celestial pole), *Xiàng-wèi* 象位 (the asterism positions), *Tiān-hàn* 天漢 (the Heavenly River — the Milky Way).

**Lower *juàn* (8 chapters)**: a separate section subtitled "*successively derived from the European books, summarizing their essentials*" (*xù jiàn Ōu-luó-bā shū, cuō qí tǐ-yào* 續見歐羅巴書，撮其體要): *Tiān-tǐ dì-dù* 天體地度 (heavenly-body and earthly-degrees), *Dù-lǐ zhī chā* 度理之差 (degree-and-principle differences), *Wěi-yào* 緯曜 (the planets), *Jīng-xiù* 經宿 (the longitude-stations), *Huáng-dào gōng-jiè* 黄道宫界 (ecliptic palace-boundaries), *Chì-dào wěi-chán* 赤道緯纒 (equatorial latitude-and-position), *Qì-hòu kè-lòu* 氣候刻漏 (seasonal-nodes and clepsydra), and an appended *Lùn rì-yuè jiāo-shí* 論日月交食 (discussion of solar-and-lunar eclipses).

The work is unusual in the late-Wànlì astronomical literature for two reasons. First, it is among the earliest Chinese-authored works (not Jesuit-collaborated) that demonstrably integrate the European nine-spheres cosmology and the European geometric account of eclipses, despite Wáng Yīngmíng's not being a Catholic convert and not being a direct member of the Beijing residence circle — documenting the indirect spread of Ricci-period European astronomy into the broader Wànlì literati culture by 1612, two decades before the *Chóngzhēn lì-shū* would formalize the synthesis. Second, the work's deliberate absence of diagrams in its original form reflects Wáng Yīngmíng's principled cosmological position — that two-dimensional representation of the spherical heavens is necessarily misleading on four specific geometric grounds (set out in Wēng Hànlín's 1646 preface).

However, what its upper-and-middle two *juàn* expound of Chinese methods is also in every case in agreement with the Western methods. For at this time, although Xú Guāngqǐ's *Xīnfǎ suànshū* had not yet emerged, Lì Mǎdòu had previously arrived in China and his teachings had been transmitted; therefore Yīngmíng tacitly used them (*Yīngmíng yīn yòng zhī ěr* 英明陰用之耳). What he discusses are all the broad outlines of astronomy; he does not match the comprehensiveness of measurement-and-computation methods later developed by Méi Wéndǐng and Xuē Fèngzuò 薛鳳祚.

However, those who study astronomy must necessarily first know the [character of] the asterisms-and-stars and the [reasons] of [their] motion, and only afterward can they investigate their precision through the degree-numbers. This book's exposition, although shallow, is indeed the doorway by which beginners enter the field.

The book opens with five diagrams. According to Wēng Hànlín's preface, Yīngmíng originally composed the book without diagrams; the present recension is from Wáng Yīngmíng's son Wáng Yǎng's reprint, in the Shùnzhì *bǐng-xū* year [1646], when Wáng Yǎng was serving as Jiāng-nán Grain-Transport Intendant — Hànlín then supplied [the diagrams]. Now examining within the book: under *Bù-tiān gē* 步天歌 chapter 1, there is an interlinear note saying "*the* Bù-tiān gē *has no good editions; this is corrected from the master, so as to be free of fish-and-tortoise [scribal] errors*" and so on. #### Original preface (Wáng Yīngmíng, 1612)

In antiquity, Jīzǐ taught the Way of the Five Cardinal Relations (which the Holy Yǔ had set in order) to King Wǔ of Zhōu — and the Five Categories ultimately end with the calendrical numbers. The calendar is the Imperial-Royal Hóng-Fàn (Great Plan) for governing-the-age; for our Confucians of practical use, [it is] our great enterprise.

What is meant by *body* (*tǐ*)? Just the *tiān-tǐ* (heaven-body): the two-pole-and-twenty-eight-mansions are the heaven-body; the seven-luminaries-and-twelve-stations are the heaven-body. As "*for things [Heaven] is not two*" — the earth-body is also the heaven-body. Of the calendar, the imaging-and-the-imparting-of-seasons is most urgent. Why have the divination-and-omen schools made [calendrics] their *fú-yìng* (lucky-correspondence) ranking? It is as though the *qīn-ruò shòu-shí* (reverent-regulation impartment-of-seasons) classic of Yáo and Shùn had been borrowed-from to support the schools of [astrologers like] Zǐ Shèn 梓慎 and Bēi Zào 禆竈! The lower-current's defects: the *Day-Officials* take the state-prohibition [against amateur calendrics] as their authority. The foolish-self [I, Yīngmíng] received the *Shàng-shū* [tradition] for generations; calendrical imaging is my hereditary occupation. I always lament that the unfamiliar avoid-and-cover [the subject] as unknowable, while the bureau-officials excuse themselves [by saying] it is not their pressing business; even those styled great Confucians of name-and-principle here often speak in dream-language. Therefore I have taken actually-measured fixed-degrees and ancient-and-modern aphorisms, abridged-them and made-a-volume, as a help to my son's classical study. All passages touching on the *bù-jīng* (uncanonical, irregular) I have one-and-all rejected. The longitude-and-latitude both raised, the broad outline is plainly visible. As for [the calendar's] applications — these are the methods of the *jī-héng* (armillary sphere) and *Líng-xiàn*, the numbers of multiplication-division-step-computation, the alternation-and-illumination — there is nothing not [governed by] ultimate-principle. [These methods] are all set out in other writings; here [I do] not detail [them].

As for those numerous-stars whose names are dependent-imitations from Qín-and-Hàn onward, all retain their old usage: why? *Names of heaven* — even the twenty-eight mansions are also borrowings. The book having been completed, [I have] in toto kept it secret in the curtain-tent, not daring to show it to others. Wànlì *rén-zǐ* [1612], ancient-Chán [Chánzhōu = Kāizhōu] Wáng Yīngmíng, *shū* (writes).

#### Postface preface (Wēng Hànlín 翁漢麐, 1646, on supplying diagrams)

Master Wáng of *Chán-yuān* came to inspect Jiāng-nán: humane, intelligent, incorruptible, decisive, what he did to make-fortune for the people cannot be counted on the fingers. Within a thousand-*lǐ* square, the joyful sound was on the road; only [Wáng] *gōng* covered-and-sheltered [the people] virtuously, and they did not know that *gōng*'s benefiting-the-people with substantial benefit was [the fruit of] his late father's bequeathing instruction with substantial learning.

The late father was broad in talent and learning; in calendrics, pitch-pipes, military affairs, agricultural-and-frontier defense, river-and-water control — there was nothing he did not deeply investigate. Outstanding [among] the great Confucians of the age. Young, he ascended the high-rank examinations; court and county both regarded him as one expected for the rank of *gōng-fǔ* (Three-Highest). But he absolutely repudiated the official career, closed his gate and cultivated the Way; the four directions' famous-and-eminent lined his door — sandals and shoes always full. He always urged: "*Today's pen-wielding houses are like the Jìn-period pure-conversers; the scholar must work for what is of use*". Therefore he developed and clarified the classics' purport, parsing-and-deciding the nature-and-fate; outside that, he simply gathered prior-dynasties' precedents and the era's pressing essentials, [in] question-and-answer.

After his hand had elaborated [the work] into a finished book, [the work] nearly filled-the-rafters. Although [it] suffered war-fires, there are still [things] preserved. Now [Wáng] *gōng* cannot bear to keep his family-treasure private; gradually [he] is bringing them to the engraving-blocks. First the *Lì-tǐ* one book — and supplying-diagrams [for it] [he] entrusted to me, [his] junior. I [though] — what should I [know of] calendrics, that I should presume? But still: in matters there is the [practice of] continuing-the-tail [adding to existing work]; in feeling, [I am] tied [to the senior]. And being honored with [Wáng] *gōng*'s command, what would I dare to refuse?

Strenuously exhausting my dullness, weighing past-and-present, measuring inches-and-tenths, comparing the round-and-square — for all the high-and-low, sparse-and-dense placements, the broad-and-narrow, longitude-and-latitude of degree-numbers — only afraid of the slightest deviation from the *qián-xiàng* (heavenly imagery), to disgrace this book.

But in my private heart there is what is unsatisfied: (1) The North Pole's elevation is 35 or 36 degrees; its position is in the north, but the diagram [necessarily] cannot but place it in the center — *first non-conformity*. (2) The heaven-body's half is below the earth, rotating-and-revolving without fixity, but the diagram cannot but lay-it-out as a static layout — *second non-conformity*. (3) The heavenly-degrees are all obliquely-divided, but the diagram [represents them] as straight rows — *third non-conformity*. (4) From the north-pole what is divided meets at the south-pole; therefore beyond the equator the degrees gradually narrow, but the diagram on the contrary spreads out at the four edges — *fourth non-conformity*.

This [is no other reason than that] the heaven-body is [a] full-round [sphere]; the four directions, up-and-down, revolve-and-return without limit; today using the limited extent of the paper to convert it to a flat circle — this is the *Gài-tiān* [cosmology] but not the *Hún-tiān* (Sphere-Heaven). The body has ten-thousand-thousand non-resemblances. I now realize that Master Wáng's composing the book without composing diagrams is not negligence; rather, the diagrams [simply] cannot be composed, so by means of *not-composing* he composed [them]. Now if [we] only enable the reader of this book to know why the *yuán* (constellation-walls) are three, why the *yě* (territories) are nine, why the *xiù* (lunar-mansions) are twenty-eight — that is graspable at one glance, [and] sufficient to be free of vague-vagueness in upward-glancing.

How should I claim that this diagram is truly sufficient to supplement this book? Only that [Wáng] *gōng* extends his family-learning to graciously instruct me, your junior, allowing me to participate in the *guǎn-kuī* (tube-peep, meaning narrow view-of-the-whole) — for this I bow my head in reverence; would [I] dare to say only one matter of submitting-and-transporting [merit, but rather to add] my voice to the multitude singing of his sheltering kindness? — Eastern Wú [Sūzhōu] Wēng Hànlín respectfully records.

### Abstract

Composition: 1612 (Wànlì *rén-zǐ* — the date both the 提要 and Wáng Yīngmíng's own preface affirm). Recension preserved: 1646 (Shùnzhì *bǐng-xū* — Wáng Yǎng's reprint with Wēng Hànlín's diagrams), reorganized in some details from Wáng Yīngmíng's original. The *Sìkù*-preserved text reflects this 1646 recension.

The work's significance:

(a) **Indirect Jesuit influence by 1612**: the 提要's careful observation that Wáng Yīngmíng "*tacitly used*" Ricci's transmitted teachings provides precious documentation of the diffusion of Jesuit astronomical doctrine into the broader Wànlì literati community within a decade-and-a-half of Ricci's settlement in Beijing (1601). Wáng Yīngmíng was not a Catholic convert and was not a member of the inner Beijing residence circle; his absorption of the European nine-spheres cosmology and the geometric account of eclipses by 1612 demonstrates that the technical content of the new astronomy — separated from its Christian theological apparatus — was already in indirect circulation among non-Catholic Chinese astronomical specialists by the late Wànlì period.

(b) **The deliberate refusal of diagrams**: Wáng Yīngmíng's principled position that two-dimensional planar diagrams of three-dimensional celestial geometry are necessarily misleading is one of the most sophisticated late-Wànlì meditations on the limits of geometric representation. Wēng Hànlín's preface preserves the four specific geometric objections — placement of the north pole, layout of the lower hemisphere, oblique-vs-straight degree-divisions, and false outward-spreading at the southern edge — and acknowledges that any 2D diagram is merely *Gài-tiān* and not *Hún-tiān*. This is, in effect, an early-modern Chinese articulation of what would later be called the cartographic projection problem: any planar projection of a sphere necessarily distorts.

(c) **Authorial humility and pedagogical purpose**: Wáng Yīngmíng's own preface is characteristically modest — he describes the work as composed for his son's classical study, "*kept secret in the curtain-tent, not daring to show it to others*". The decision to print only came posthumously through his son. This locates the work in the late-Wànlì tradition of family-pedagogical scholarship — not unrelated to but distinct from the more formal scholar-bureaucrat publications.

The 提要's comparison of Wáng Yīngmíng to the early-Qīng mathematicians Méi Wéndǐng and Xuē Fèngzuò 薛鳳祚 — "*[Wáng's exposition] does not match the comprehensiveness of measurement-and-computation methods later developed by [these]*" — frames the work as preparatory rather than mature: a doorway (*mén-jìng* 門徑) for beginners but not the complete mathematical system. This is the standard late-imperial Chinese assessment of the late-Wànlì transitional astronomical literature.

For the broader Wànlì astronomical context, see [[KR3f0008]] *Gǔ-jīn lǜ-lì kǎo* (the contemporary calendar-reform agitation), [[KR3f0009]] *Qián-kūn tǐ-yì* (Ricci's foundational cosmographic primer), and [[KR3f0013]] *Xīnfǎ suànshū* (the eventual systematic synthesis). For Wáng Yīngmíng's biography, see [[王英明]].

## Other points of interest

The 提要's observation that Wáng Yīngmíng's lower *juàn* explicitly cites European books (the chapter being subtitled "*successively derived from the European books, summarizing their essentials*") is documentation of an important transitional moment: by 1612, an independent Chinese scholar in a provincial location can openly reference "European books" as a recognized source-category. The phrase *Ōu-luó-bā shū* 歐羅巴書 ("books from Europe") was still novel in Chinese writing of the period; Wáng's casual deployment of it suggests it had already become acceptable terminology for the educated provincial reader.

Wáng Yīngmíng's preface includes an early-modern critique of Chinese state-astronomy's institutional secrecy ("*the lower-current's defects: the Day-Officials take the state-prohibition [against amateur calendrics] as their authority*") — a striking late-Wànlì call for the democratization of astronomical knowledge that aligns Wáng with the broader contemporary critique of the *Qīntiān-jiān*'s institutional inadequacies that motivated the 1611 calendar-reform memorial.
